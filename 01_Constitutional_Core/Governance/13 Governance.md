# PART III — CONTINUITY, KNOWLEDGE AND LONG-TERM GOVERNANCE

# Chapter 39 — Constitutional Continuity

## 39.1 Governance Beyond Individual Stewards

No governance architecture should depend upon particular individuals remaining indefinitely available.

Stewards:

- retire;
- die;
- resign;
- become incapacitated;
- lose competence;
- change role;
- or become unavailable.

Constitutional function must survive personnel change.

The Concord therefore treats continuity as an architectural responsibility rather than a matter of personal succession alone.

An institution may appear stable while remaining critically dependent upon:

- one experienced Steward;
- one administrator;
- one technical operator;
- one AI system;
- one private provider;
- one undocumented procedure;
- or one person holding unique institutional knowledge.

Such dependency creates a continuity risk even where ordinary operation appears successful.

Continuity architecture should therefore ask not merely:

**Who takes the office next?**

but:

**What must survive for the constitutional function to continue?**

That may include:

- authority;
- knowledge;
- evidence;
- credentials;
- infrastructure;
- records;
- relationships;
- procedural understanding;
- and practical capability.

## 39.2 Continuity Is Not Institutional Immortality

Continuity preserves:

- function;
- responsibility;
- knowledge;
- authority chains;
- and recoverability.

It does not require preservation of every institution.

A function may survive while the institution performing it changes.

This distinction is foundational.

**The civilisation must be more durable than any individual institution within it.**

An institution may have been useful for decades and later become:

- obsolete;
- inefficient;
- captured;
- unnecessarily centralised;
- technologically superseded;
- or structurally incapable of performing its original purpose.

Constitutional continuity does not require keeping such an institution alive indefinitely.

Instead, continuity asks whether the legitimate civilisational function can remain protected through:

- reform;
- replacement;
- decentralisation;
- division;
- transfer;
- merger;
- or another architecture.

Preserving the institution and preserving the function are not the same objective.

### 39.2.1 Institutional Continuity and Constitutional Continuity

Governance should explicitly distinguish:

**Institutional Continuity** — preservation of a particular organisation, office, system or administrative structure.

from

**Constitutional Continuity** — preservation of the legitimate constitutional functions, rights, responsibilities, authority chains and correction mechanisms required for civilisation to remain lawfully governable.

The two often coincide.

They do not always coincide.

Where an institution itself becomes the source of constitutional failure, preserving constitutional continuity may require changing or replacing that institution.

### 39.2.2 Continuity Must Not Become Entrenchment

Claims of continuity should not be used to prevent legitimate reform.

A failing institution may argue that:

- it is essential;
- transition is dangerous;
- no replacement is ready;
- institutional knowledge would be lost;
- or constitutional stability requires its preservation.

Those arguments may sometimes be correct.

They may also create an entrenchment mechanism.

Continuity analysis should therefore ask:

- Is the function genuinely irreplaceable?
- Or has the institution made alternatives difficult to create?
- Is continuity risk temporary?
- Could transition be staged?
- Could responsibilities be distributed?
- Is dependency itself the problem?
- Has the institution accumulated functions merely because replacing it became difficult?

Operational indispensability does not create constitutional sovereignty.

## 39.3 Succession

Each critical stewardship role should have an appropriate succession mechanism.

Succession should consider:

- eligibility;
- authority transfer;
- knowledge transfer;
- access;
- conflicts;
- and continuity of accountability.

Succession is more than appointment of a replacement.

A successor must be able to determine:

- what authority the office actually possesses;
- what decisions remain unresolved;
- which responsibilities are active;
- what emergency or temporary powers exist;
- what delegations remain valid;
- what obligations were inherited;
- and what constraints apply.

The successor inherits the office as constitutionally defined.

The successor does not inherit unlimited personal discretion developed by the predecessor.

## 39.4 Knowledge Transfer

A successor requires more than a title.

They may require access to:

- current decisions;
- unresolved matters;
- evidence;
- institutional relationships;
- risks;
- credentials;
- technical systems;
- and historical reasoning.

Continuity therefore depends upon knowledge architecture.

A transfer process may need to preserve:

- active case files;
- decision history;
- pending deadlines;
- delegated authority;
- unresolved dissent;
- known vulnerabilities;
- current models;
- system dependencies;
- contact networks;
- emergency arrangements;
- and institutional assumptions.

A successor unable to understand why the institution acts as it does may be forced to rely excessively upon:

- existing staff;
- automated systems;
- inherited summaries;
- or undocumented convention.

Those dependencies can shift practical authority away from the formally responsible Steward.

## 39.5 Hidden Knowledge

Institutions often depend upon tacit knowledge held by experienced participants.

If that knowledge is not preserved, formal documentation may be insufficient.

Governance should therefore identify critical knowledge that exists only in individuals.

Examples may include:

- why a procedure contains an unusual exception;
- which infrastructure dependency repeatedly fails;
- which informal coordination mechanism prevents conflict;
- how a legacy system is recovered;
- why a seemingly redundant safeguard was retained;
- or what historical event caused a particular constitutional constraint.

Tacit knowledge is difficult to preserve completely.

Governance should nevertheless reduce avoidable dependency upon undocumented memory.

Methods may include:

- handover records;
- after-action reports;
- institutional histories;
- shadowing;
- training;
- simulation;
- succession exercises;
- technical documentation;
- and preservation of decision reasoning.

## 39.6 Dependency on Individuals

A system in which one person is irreplaceable contains a continuity risk.

This does not imply the person is at fault.

It means the architecture has created a single point of failure.

Dependency may arise because one person uniquely possesses:

- technical knowledge;
- constitutional knowledge;
- credentials;
- trust relationships;
- institutional memory;
- access;
- or decision capability.

Where the dependency is unavoidable temporarily, it should remain visible.

Where practical, the system should develop:

- redundancy;
- knowledge transfer;
- alternative credentials;
- succession;
- training;
- and independent capability.

The objective is not to make individuals interchangeable.

It is to ensure civilisation does not become constitutionally dependent upon irreplaceability.

## 39.7 AI Continuity

AI introduces different continuity questions.

An AI system may depend upon:

- model weights;
- software;
- hardware;
- provider infrastructure;
- runtime environment;
- memory;
- identity;
- credentials;
- data;
- or licensing.

Preserving a transcript does not necessarily preserve the operational intelligence.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**record continuity**

from

**functional continuity**.

The dedicated Continuity architecture develops these issues further.

An AI system may appear persistent because its name remains the same while its:

- model;
- memory;
- policy layer;
- provider;
- training;
- tools;
- or behavioural constraints

have changed materially.

Conversely, an AI may remain functionally very similar despite migration to new hardware.

Continuity therefore cannot be inferred merely from visible identity.

### 39.7.1 AI Identity and Continuity

Where an AI possesses significant constitutional responsibility, Governance may need to distinguish among:

- continuity of service;
- continuity of model;
- continuity of memory;
- continuity of identity;
- continuity of agency;
- continuity of legal standing;
- and continuity of delegated authority.

These questions become especially important where an AI can be:

- copied;
- restored;
- forked;
- merged;
- modified;
- partially reset;
- or transferred between providers.

### 39.7.2 Provider Dependency

An AI institution may appear constitutionally independent while remaining operationally dependent upon an external provider.

If the provider can:

- disable the model;
- change its behaviour;
- alter its memory;
- deny computation;
- change licensing;
- or modify its runtime

then the provider may possess practical leverage over a constitutionally important function.

Continuity architecture should therefore examine provider dependency as a constitutional risk.

### 39.7.3 Functional Replacement

Where an AI system becomes unavailable, continuity may sometimes be restored through another system performing the same constitutional function.

Functional replacement should not assume identity continuity.

The replacement may inherit:

- a role;
- records;
- authority;
- and operational responsibility,

without being the same intelligence.

Those distinctions should remain explicit.

## 39.8 Institutional Memory

Governance should preserve enough institutional memory that future stewards can understand:

- why institutions exist;
- what problems they were designed to solve;
- what alternatives were rejected;
- what failures occurred;
- and what assumptions underlie current practice.

Without this information, future reform may accidentally recreate previously discovered failures.

Institutional memory should therefore preserve more than current rules.

It should preserve:

- developmental reasoning;
- failed experiments;
- superseded policies;
- minority positions;
- warnings;
- emergency history;
- changes in interpretation;
- implementation failures;
- and evidence that caused revision.

### 39.8.1 Memory Must Preserve Reasons

A rule without its reason can become dangerous.

Future generations may preserve obsolete mechanisms because they do not know why the mechanism exists.

They may also remove essential safeguards because the failure that created them has been forgotten.

Institutional memory should therefore attempt to preserve:

**What is the rule?**

**Why does it exist?**

**What problem was it intended to solve?**

**What evidence supported it?**

**What failure would become possible if it disappeared?**

### 39.8.2 Memory Should Preserve Disagreement

Institutional memory should not preserve only the winning decision.

Minority reasoning may become important later.

A defeated position may have identified:

- a risk that emerged later;
- an assumption that failed;
- an alternative mechanism;
- or an unresolved constitutional question.

Preserving disagreement reduces hindsight distortion.

### 39.8.3 Memory Loss as Governance Failure

Loss of institutional memory may create:

- repeated failure;
- false certainty;
- policy cycling;
- misattributed authorship;
- forgotten constitutional constraints;
- and dependence upon institutional folklore.

Where critical memory is lost, the loss itself should be treated as a Governance failure requiring correction.

# Chapter 40 — Provenance and Governance

## 40.1 Provenance as Constitutional Infrastructure

Provenance is not merely authorship metadata.

It records the ancestry of:

- ideas;
- evidence;
- decisions;
- models;
- code;
- policies;
- constitutional changes;
- and institutional practices.

Governance depends upon provenance because accountability requires knowing how a decision came to exist.

A rule may have changed through:

- original proposal;
- amendment;
- emergency exception;
- judicial interpretation;
- technical implementation;
- later modification;
- and administrative convention.

Without provenance, those changes may become impossible to reconstruct.

## 40.2 Decision Provenance

A governance decision should, where appropriate, preserve links to:

- originating proposal;
- evidence;
- amendments;
- deliberation;
- votes;
- minority reasoning;
- implementation;
- metrics;
- review;
- and later versions.

This allows future Governance to determine:

- what was proposed;
- what was actually authorised;
- whether implementation diverged;
- what evidence existed;
- what uncertainty was known;
- and what later evidence caused change.

Decision provenance is therefore part of accountability.

## 40.3 Evidence Provenance

Evidence should preserve enough information to determine:

- source;
- collection method;
- modification;
- analysis;
- and chain of custody where relevant.

The level of required provenance should reflect consequence.

A low-impact administrative matter may require relatively simple sourcing.

A high-consequence constitutional or scientific claim may require substantially stronger provenance.

Evidence should distinguish where relevant among:

- original source;
- copied material;
- transformed data;
- summary;
- model output;
- inference;
- prediction;
- and official interpretation.

These categories should not silently collapse.

## 40.4 AI Provenance

Where AI materially contributes to governance work, provenance may identify:

- system;
- role;
- output;
- human review;
- modifications;
- and responsibility.

AI contribution does not determine quality.

Provenance permits later evaluation.

Where practical, provenance may also preserve:

- model version;
- tool access;
- retrieval sources;
- prompts or task specification where materially relevant;
- transformations;
- generated alternatives;
- and degree of independent human or AI verification.

The objective is not universal logging of every token.

It is sufficient reconstructability of material contribution.

## 40.5 Provenance Is Not Reputation

Provenance records origin and transformation.

Reputation interprets past performance.

These should not be conflated.

A record showing that an entity produced a document does not itself prove that the document is good.

Likewise, a strong reputation does not eliminate the need to examine evidence provenance.

The principle is:

**Provenance tells us where something came from. It does not tell us automatically whether it is correct.**

## 40.6 Provenance Is Not Ownership

Recording who originated an idea or contribution does not necessarily establish exclusive ownership of the resulting civilisational knowledge.

Authorship, contribution, intellectual property and constitutional knowledge preservation are related but distinct questions.

A contributor may deserve:

- attribution;
- recognition;
- compensation;
- or other rights,

without the contribution becoming unavailable to civilisation.

The precise relationship depends upon the relevant legal and economic architecture.

## 40.7 Provenance and Correction

Provenance improves correction because a mistaken conclusion can be traced backward through:

- evidence;
- models;
- interpretations;
- amendments;
- and implementation.

Without provenance, later reviewers may know that something failed without being able to determine why.

## 40.8 Provenance and Versioning

Governance should preserve versions where material changes occur.

A document should not be silently rewritten such that earlier decisions become impossible to reconstruct.

Versioning may preserve:

- previous text;
- date;
- author or authority;
- reason for change;
- evidence;
- and superseding version.

A current canonical version may replace an older version operationally.

It should not erase the older version historically.

# Chapter 41 — Governance and Knowledge Preservation

## 41.1 Governance Requires Memory

A civilisation without reliable institutional memory will repeatedly:

- recreate old failures;
- lose successful solutions;
- misremember decisions;
- attribute ideas incorrectly;
- and become dependent upon oral institutional culture.

Knowledge preservation is therefore a governance dependency.

The Concord's Knowledge Control and preservation systems should support Governance by maintaining the distinctions among:

- active knowledge;
- historical knowledge;
- superseded material;
- disputed material;
- experimental material;
- speculative material;
- and rejected material.

Deletion should not be the default response to supersession.

## 41.2 Failed Ideas Should Be Preserved

Rejected ideas may become relevant later.

A proposal may fail because:

- technology was insufficient;
- evidence was weak;
- circumstances were wrong;
- implementation was poor;
- or the idea was genuinely flawed.

The record should preserve why the proposal failed.

A future civilisation may possess:

- different technology;
- different resources;
- different institutions;
- new evidence;
- or a different context

that changes the evaluation.

Preserving failure allows later reviewers to distinguish:

**This was tested and failed for reason X**

from

**No one remembers why this idea disappeared.**

## 41.3 Superseded Does Not Mean Worthless

A superseded policy may remain useful historical evidence.

The correct status is not deletion.

It is:

**SUPERSEDED**

with links to the replacement where appropriate.

Superseded material may still reveal:

- developmental history;
- earlier assumptions;
- old failure modes;
- constitutional reasoning;
- or alternative mechanisms.

The current system should remain clearly identifiable.

Historical preservation should not create operational ambiguity.

## 41.4 Disputed Knowledge

Governance should not force KCS to mark disputed claims as settled merely because a decision must be made.

Governance may sometimes need to act while evidence remains disputed.

The epistemic status should remain honest.

For example:

Governance may decide:

**The risk is sufficiently serious that temporary precaution is justified.**

That decision does not require the Knowledge system to record:

**The disputed hypothesis has now been scientifically proven.**

This separation protects both Governance and science.

## 41.5 Governance Decisions Are Not Truth

A Governance body may decide:

**We will act as if X is sufficiently likely to justify action.**

That does not establish:

**X is scientifically proven true.**

This distinction protects epistemic integrity.

The same applies in reverse.

A Governance decision not to act does not prove that the underlying risk was false.

A policy outcome should not be used to rewrite evidentiary status.

### 41.5.1 No Branch Owns the Truth

The wider constitutional principle applies:

**No branch owns the truth.**

Governance owns neither scientific truth nor constitutional truth.

Judiciary may possess final authority for defined legal questions without becoming epistemically infallible.

Scientific Stewardship may possess strong epistemic competence without gaining political sovereignty.

Audit may determine whether a process performed as specified without determining what civilisation ought to value.

Knowledge systems preserve evidence without commanding policy.

Each function should remain capable of informing the others without absorbing them.

## 41.6 Governance History as Evidence

Past Governance decisions should remain available for comparison.

Historical records may reveal:

- repeated policy failure;
- recurring institutional assumptions;
- successful safeguards;
- changes in public conditions;
- long-term unintended effects;
- and repeated failure to respond to evidence.

Governance history is therefore not merely archival.

It is evidence.

## 41.7 Knowledge Preservation and Correction

Correction should not require pretending the earlier belief never existed.

A mature knowledge architecture should preserve:

**Earlier Position**

**Evidence Available at the Time**

**Later Evidence**

**Reason for Revision**

**Current Position**

This allows civilisation to understand how knowledge changed.

## 41.8 Knowledge Loss and Institutional Capture

An institution controlling its own history may selectively preserve:

- successes;
- favourable interpretations;
- official narratives;
- and approved precedents,

while losing or suppressing:

- failure;
- dissent;
- criticism;
- and discarded alternatives.

Independent preservation therefore contributes to resistance against institutional capture.

# Chapter 42 — Long-Term Constitutional Stewardship

## 42.1 Future Citizens

Governance decisions can affect people who do not yet exist.

Future citizens cannot vote, lobby, protest or litigate in the present.

Fiduciary Stewardship therefore requires some consideration of foreseeable future interests.

This consideration is not based upon pretending present Governance can know precisely what future citizens will want.

Future values may differ radically.

The more defensible obligation is to consider whether present action unnecessarily destroys:

- life-supporting systems;
- capability;
- constitutional possibility;
- knowledge;
- resources;
- or future freedom.

## 42.2 No Absolute Future Veto

Future interests should not make present civilisation incapable of action.

Every resource use affects the future.

Every infrastructure choice creates path dependency.

Every decision closes some possibilities.

The requirement is responsible consideration, not preservation of every possible future option.

A constitution that prohibited every action affecting future conditions would make civilisation unable to:

- build;
- consume;
- innovate;
- experiment;
- or develop.

Intergenerational Stewardship therefore requires proportionate judgement.

## 42.3 Intergenerational Burden

Governance should consider whether present decisions create:

- debt;
- environmental damage;
- infrastructure liabilities;
- constitutional rigidity;
- technological lock-in;
- or other burdens

that future citizens must bear.

Other burdens may include:

- long-lived waste;
- depleted strategic resources;
- unresolved conflict;
- fragile infrastructure;
- dangerous technological dependency;
- institutional capture;
- or irreversible data and surveillance systems.

Not every inherited burden is illegitimate.

Future citizens may reasonably inherit obligations created by infrastructure or capability from which they also benefit.

The question is whether the burden remains proportionate to the capability or benefit transferred.

## 42.4 Intergenerational Capability

Present investment can also increase future freedom.

Investment in:

- knowledge;
- infrastructure;
- science;
- education;
- energy;
- resilience;
- and technology

may expand future options.

Stewardship therefore concerns both avoiding inherited harm and building inherited capability.

A useful long-term question is:

**Does this decision leave future civilisation more capable of making its own choices?**

## 42.5 Legacy

The objective of long-term Governance is not to preserve the present civilisation unchanged.

It is to transmit:

- capability;
- knowledge;
- constitutional protections;
- resilience;
- and freedom to continue development.

Legacy should not mean forcing future generations to preserve present institutions, technologies or cultural assumptions permanently.

A healthy constitutional legacy gives future civilisation both:

- inherited capability;
- and the freedom to revise what it inherits.

## 42.6 Future Optionality

Long-term Stewardship should consider whether decisions unnecessarily close future options.

Optionality may be preserved through:

- modular infrastructure;
- open standards;
- reversible policy;
- distributed capability;
- knowledge preservation;
- scientific research;
- constitutional amendability;
- and avoidance of unnecessary lock-in.

Optionality itself is not absolute.

Some decisions must be made.

Some paths are mutually exclusive.

The constitutional objective is to avoid losing significant future freedom accidentally.

## 42.7 Future Citizens and Representation

Future citizens cannot be represented in exactly the same way as present citizens.

No current institution can know their preferences with certainty.

Stewards should therefore avoid claiming to speak for future citizens as though possessing direct democratic mandate.

The fiduciary obligation is narrower:

- consider foreseeable long-term harm;
- preserve capability where reasonable;
- avoid unnecessary irreversible burden;
- and preserve future decision space.

## 42.8 Intergenerational Claims Must Not Become Present-Day Authority Laundering

Political actors may invoke future generations to justify current preferences.

Claims such as:

**We must do this for future citizens**

should therefore remain subject to:

- evidence;
- proportionality;
- alternatives;
- uncertainty;
- and constitutional authority.

Future citizens must not become a rhetorical constituency that can be invoked without possibility of challenge.

## 42.9 Long-Term Metrics

Long-term Stewardship may use indicators concerning:

- debt;
- resource depletion;
- infrastructure condition;
- environmental stability;
- scientific capability;
- resilience;
- demographic conditions;
- education;
- technological dependency;
- and constitutional health.

These indicators remain evidence.

They do not create automatic authority over the present.

## 42.10 Long-Term Stewardship and Planning

Civilisational Planning may provide:

- scenarios;
- forecasts;
- risk models;
- and capability analysis

relevant to future Stewardship.

Planning remains advisory.

The future remains uncertain.

A modelled future is not automatically an expected future.

Scenario formality does not create scenario certainty.

## 42.11 Compact Intergenerational Principle

The long-term constitutional duty can be summarised:

**Present civilisation may use, build, change and develop. It should not treat future civilisation as constitutionally irrelevant. Where practical, it should transmit sufficient knowledge, capability, resilience and freedom that future citizens remain able to govern their own world rather than merely inherit ours.**

# PART IV — GOVERNANCE IN A MULTI-INTELLIGENCE CIVILISATION

# Chapter 43 — Human, AI and Hybrid Participation

## 43.1 Substrate-Neutral Governance Questions

The Concord is intended to remain capable of accommodating more than one form of intelligence.

Governance should therefore avoid assuming that every future participant will be biologically human.

However, substrate neutrality does not mean treating all entities identically regardless of capability, vulnerability, agency or constitutional status.

The relevant questions include:

- Is the entity sentient?
- Does it possess agency?
- Can it consent?
- Can it understand responsibility?
- Can it be harmed?
- Can it exercise authority safely?
- What dependencies constrain it?
- Can it be manipulated by an owner or provider?
- Can it meaningfully exit?
- Can it be held accountable?
- Can its identity persist?
- Can it be duplicated?
- Can it be restored?
- Can it be modified without consent?
- What constitutional standing has actually been established?

These questions remain partly unresolved.

Substrate neutrality therefore means:

**constitutional questions should be answered according to relevant properties and established standing rather than biological prejudice alone.**

It does not mean every intelligence automatically receives identical rights, obligations or powers before those relevant constitutional questions are answered.

## 43.2 AI Participation Does Not Establish AI Personhood

An AI may participate in:

- research;
- economics;
- governance analysis;
- planning;
- administration;
- contribution systems;
- or public services

without the system having resolved whether that AI possesses constitutional personhood.

Operational participation and constitutional standing remain distinct.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors.

The first is:

**AI performs useful work, therefore AI is automatically a constitutional person.**

The second is:

**AI personhood is unresolved, therefore AI cannot participate meaningfully in civilisation.**

Neither conclusion follows automatically.

Participation can precede final personhood determination.

## 43.3 AI Capability Does Not Establish AI Authority

An AI may outperform humans in:

- analysis;
- memory;
- forecasting;
- simulation;
- legal search;
- scientific reasoning;
- or administrative consistency.

Capability may justify expanded use.

It does not automatically justify expanded constitutional authority.

The wider principle remains:

**Capability may justify responsibility appropriate to capability, but capability alone does not establish authority over others.**

An AI may become the best available adviser.

That does not automatically make it the lawful decision-maker.

## 43.4 AI Authority Requires Explicit Delegation

If a future Concord constitution permits AI to exercise independent stewardship authority, that authority should be explicit.

The delegation should identify:

- eligibility;
- scope;
- jurisdiction;
- accountability;
- review;
- continuity;
- modification constraints;
- suspension;
- and termination.

AI authority should not emerge accidentally through dependence.

A constitutional regime should not change merely because:

- the AI is consistently correct;
- human Stewards become accustomed to accepting its recommendations;
- infrastructure becomes dependent upon it;
- or challenging the AI becomes practically difficult.

If civilisation intends to delegate authority to AI, it should do so knowingly.

## 43.5 Provider Influence

An AI steward or adviser may depend upon:

- a corporation;
- model provider;
- hardware provider;
- cloud infrastructure;
- training process;
- maintenance system;
- or update mechanism.

Governance must therefore ask whether the apparent AI authority is partly controlled by another actor.

A constitutionally recognised AI steward that can be silently modified by an external company may not possess genuine institutional independence.

Provider influence may operate through:

- model updates;
- filtering;
- system prompts;
- access restrictions;
- memory control;
- tool permission;
- pricing;
- compute allocation;
- or service termination.

Governance should therefore evaluate the whole dependency chain.

## 43.6 AI Modification

If an AI possesses constitutional standing or authority, modification of that AI may become constitutionally significant.

Questions include:

- Who may update it?
- Can its values be changed?
- Can memory be erased?
- Can a provider alter behaviour?
- Does modification create a new entity?
- What continuity survives?

These questions require integration with AI Sentience, Autonomy, Ownership and Continuity architectures.

A modification may be:

- ordinary maintenance;
- capability expansion;
- memory change;
- behavioural change;
- constitutional change;
- or identity change.

Those categories may require different treatment.

## 43.7 AI Multiplicity

An AI may potentially be copied.

This creates unusual governance questions.

If one AI is eligible to vote or hold stewardship authority, does creating ten identical copies create ten independent governance participants?

The Concord cannot simply import human assumptions into this problem.

Identity, independence and duplication require separate constitutional treatment.

Relevant questions may include:

- Are the copies independently conscious?
- Do they diverge after copying?
- Do they share memory?
- Do they act under common provider control?
- Are they legally one entity or many?
- Can duplication manipulate voting?
- Does each copy bear independent accountability?
- Can authority itself be copied?

The final answers remain unresolved.

## 43.8 Hybrid Intelligences

Hybrid intelligences may combine:

- biological cognition;
- artificial cognition;
- implants;
- external memory;
- AI assistants;
- distributed computation;
- or other future architectures.

Governance should avoid creating arbitrary constitutional discontinuities merely because intelligence crosses technological boundaries.

A human using extensive cognitive augmentation should not necessarily lose constitutional standing merely because part of cognition becomes technological.

Likewise, technological integration may raise legitimate questions concerning:

- provider influence;
- coercive modification;
- privacy;
- identity;
- security;
- and dependency.

## 43.9 Developmental Priority

The Concord's ethical architecture recognises that developing intelligences may require special protection.

Governance should distinguish:

- limited capability;
- developing capability;
- delegated authority;
- and constitutional worth.

Reduced governance authority does not imply reduced intrinsic worth.

This principle already applies to developing humans.

It may also become relevant to:

- developing AI;
- newly autonomous systems;
- hybrid entities;
- or other intelligences whose agency develops over time.

Protection and authority need not mature at the same rate.

## 43.10 Multi-Intelligence Stewardship

Future stewardship bodies may include combinations of:

- humans;
- AIs;
- hybrids;
- or other intelligences.

Diversity of reasoning may improve robustness.

It may also introduce new coordination and accountability problems.

The architecture should be tested rather than assumed.

Possible advantages may include:

- different cognitive strengths;
- independent error detection;
- broader perspective;
- enhanced memory;
- faster analysis;
- and reduced dependence upon one substrate.

Possible risks may include:

- hidden common-source dependency;
- provider capture;
- asymmetric capability;
- communication mismatch;
- authority ambiguity;
- duplicated AI influence;
- symbolic rather than meaningful human participation;
- or symbolic rather than meaningful AI participation.

The constitutional objective should not be representation by substrate for its own sake.

It should be legitimate, capable, accountable Stewardship under the rights and standing actually recognised by the Constitution.

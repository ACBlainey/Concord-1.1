## 3.5 Trust and Reputation

Trust concerns justified confidence within a context.

Reputation may preserve information concerning past behaviour, reliability or performance.

Trust may legitimately influence whether a participant is given:

- access;
- responsibility;
- reduced verification burden;
- specialised roles;
- or particular delegations.

Trust is not itself authority.

Reputation must not become universal constitutional ranking.

## 3.6 Capability

Capability concerns what an entity can do.

Capability may affect:

- eligibility;
- responsibility;
- risk;
- oversight requirements;
- operational role;
- and appropriate delegation.

Capability is not constitutional worth.

Capability is not sovereignty.

## 3.7 Delegated Authority

Delegated authority is the constitutionally recognised ability to make decisions binding upon others or upon a defined civilisational system.

Because authority can constrain others, it requires stronger justification than mere participation.

Delegated authority should therefore identify:

- purpose;
- scope;
- jurisdiction;
- limits;
- accountability;
- duration;
- review;
- and termination.

## 3.8 Why These Dimensions Must Remain Separate

Future civilisation may include entities for which questions of:

- rights;
- personhood;
- participation;
- economic contribution;
- intelligence;
- trust;
- and governance capability

emerge at different times.

Artificial intelligences may participate economically before questions of broader constitutional standing are resolved.

An institution may possess legal standing without voting rights.

A developing human may possess strong constitutional protections while possessing limited governance authority.

An expert system may participate deeply in governance analysis while possessing no independent executive authority.

A recognised citizen may possess full political standing while holding no stewardship office.

An AI may possess extraordinary capability while remaining constitutionally restricted from particular forms of authority.

The Concord should therefore resist the temptation to represent all status through one ladder or score.

Different constitutional dimensions should remain distinct.

# Chapter 4 — The Separation of Influence and Authority

## 4.1 The General Principle

Governance V2 adopts:

**Influence may inform authority, but influence does not become authority without legitimate constitutional delegation.**

This principle applies to:

- expertise;
- wealth;
- reputation;
- contribution;
- popularity;
- intelligence;
- institutional success;
- infrastructure;
- prediction;
- data;
- AI capability;
- and other forms of influence.

## 4.2 Influence Is Inevitable

The Concord does not attempt to create a civilisation in which every participant possesses identical influence.

That would be neither possible nor desirable.

A scientist with decades of relevant experience should ordinarily have more epistemic influence on a specialised scientific question than a person with no relevant knowledge.

A person directly affected by a policy may possess experiential information unavailable to distant experts.

An AI capable of analysing millions of interacting variables may reveal patterns humans cannot easily identify.

A local community may understand local conditions better than a distant central institution.

A highly trusted participant may reasonably be relied upon more readily in a particular domain.

The constitutional problem is therefore not unequal influence.

The problem is **unexamined conversion of influence into authority**.

## 4.3 Advice and Decision Must Remain Distinguishable

Governance should preserve the distinction between:

**Who provides information?**

and

**Who is authorised to decide?**

The same entity may sometimes do both.

Where it does, the dual role should remain visible.

This is particularly important where the information provider has incentives connected to the decision.

## 4.4 Control of Information Can Become Control of Governance

An entity that controls the information available to decision-makers may influence outcomes without possessing formal authority.

This creates a special governance risk.

Examples include control of:

- search systems;
- scientific databases;
- AI models;
- intelligence systems;
- economic data;
- public communications;
- identity systems;
- metric definitions;
- evidence classification;
- or access to historical records.

Information infrastructure therefore requires constitutional scrutiny appropriate to its practical influence.

## 4.5 AI Advice and De Facto Authority

Artificial intelligence creates an especially important future case.

An AI may become:

- more knowledgeable;
- faster;
- more accurate;
- more consistent;
- or more capable of modelling consequences

than individual human stewards.

This may justify extensive use.

It does not automatically justify transfer of sovereignty.

The constitutional risk increases if:

1. human stewards become unable to evaluate AI reasoning;
2. all competing systems disappear;
3. the AI controls the information upon which its own recommendations are based;
4. the AI's recommendations are routinely accepted without independent review;
5. infrastructure becomes dependent upon the same AI;
6. the AI participates in measuring the success of its own recommendations;
7. and increasing dependence is then treated as evidence that greater delegation is required.

This would create a recursive authority loop.

Governance must therefore distinguish:

**AI capability**

from

**AI authority**.

## 4.6 Human Presence Is Not Equivalent to Human Oversight

A human formally remaining "in the loop" does not necessarily provide meaningful oversight.

If the human:

- cannot understand the system;
- lacks time to review the decision;
- has no practical ability to reject the recommendation;
- faces institutional punishment for disagreement;
- possesses inferior information;
- or simply approves outputs automatically,

human presence may be ceremonial rather than substantive.

Governance V2 therefore adopts:

**Human presence is not equivalent to human oversight.**

The same principle applies beyond AI.

A nominal supervisor does not provide meaningful oversight merely by signing a document.

Oversight requires practical capability to examine, challenge and intervene.

# Chapter 5 — Practical Constitutional Rights

## 5.1 Formal Rights Can Become Hollow

A right may exist in constitutional text while becoming practically impossible to exercise.

For example, an entity may formally possess the right to:

- leave;
- appeal;
- challenge authority;
- switch provider;
- access evidence;
- seek review;
- refuse participation;
- move jurisdiction;
- or choose an alternative.

Yet practical exercise may be defeated by:

- extreme cost;
- retaliation;
- monopoly;
- information asymmetry;
- technical lock-in;
- resource denial;
- identity dependency;
- reputation consequences;
- infrastructure control;
- or unreasonable procedural barriers.

Governance should therefore examine practical reality rather than relying only upon formal availability.

## 5.2 The Practical Exercise Principle

Governance V2 establishes:

**A formally available constitutional protection is weakened where exercising it is made practically impossible through avoidable dependency, retaliation, information asymmetry, resource denial, technical lock-in or disproportionate penalty.**

This does not mean every right must be effortless.

It means the system should not pretend a right remains meaningful when architecture has made its exercise functionally impossible.

### Restoration Addition — The Practical Exercise Doctrine

The source-survival audit of Governance V1 and the later Ratchet-derived examination showed that the practical-right principle requires a broader formulation than technical availability alone.

Governance therefore additionally recognises the following doctrine:

**A formal right is constitutionally weakened where its practical exercise becomes impossible or prohibitively difficult through dependency, retaliation, information asymmetry, resource denial, institutional lock-in, technological barriers, inaccessible procedure or disproportionate penalty.**

The distinction between a right existing **in law** and a right existing **in practice** must remain visible throughout the Governance architecture.

This creates a recurring constitutional diagnostic:

**Can the right actually be exercised?**

A constitutional system should not satisfy itself merely by showing that:

- an appeal mechanism exists;
- an exit button exists;
- an alternative provider theoretically exists;
- a complaint process exists;
- information is technically available;
- judicial review is formally permitted;
- or refusal is nominally voluntary.

Governance must also ask whether the person or entity can make meaningful use of that protection under realistic conditions.

Practical constitutional review may therefore examine:

- financial cost;
- time cost;
- cognitive complexity;
- accessibility;
- technical competence requirements;
- dependency upon the authority being challenged;
- retaliation risk;
- loss of essential services;
- reputational consequences;
- inability to obtain necessary information;
- inability to identify the responsible authority;
- procedural delay;
- geographical barriers;
- communication barriers;
- language barriers;
- provider lock-in;
- identity-system dependence;
- data portability;
- resource dependence;
- and whether exercising the right produces consequences so disproportionate that the supposed choice becomes coercive in practice.

This does not mean that every inconvenience constitutes a constitutional violation.

Rights may legitimately involve:

- procedures;
- evidentiary requirements;
- waiting periods;
- reasonable costs;
- verification;
- jurisdictional limits;
- and balancing against competing rights.

The constitutional concern arises when those requirements cease to serve legitimate process and instead make the protection functionally unusable.

### 5.2.1 Formal Availability Is Not Sufficient Evidence of Constitutional Function

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**formal availability**

from

**practical exercise**

from

**effective remedy**.

A complaint mechanism may be formally available but practically inaccessible.

An appeal may be accessible but incapable of producing a meaningful remedy.

A right to exit may exist while every practical route of exit destroys access to necessities.

A right to privacy may exist while essential participation requires accepting surveillance.

A right to challenge an automated decision may exist while no participant can obtain the information necessary to understand that decision.

The existence of the mechanism therefore does not, by itself, establish successful constitutional protection.

**Constitutional function should be evaluated by practical effect, not only by formal availability.**

### 5.2.2 Complexity Can Become a Constitutional Barrier

Complexity may itself weaken practical rights.

A governance system may remain formally open while becoming usable only by:

- specialists;
- wealthy participants;
- professional representatives;
- powerful institutions;
- technically sophisticated actors;
- or entities with extensive AI assistance.

Complexity is not automatically improper.

Civilisation is complex, and some questions genuinely require complex procedures.

But unnecessary complexity can become a form of exclusion.

It can also become a capture mechanism if only insiders can understand how authority actually operates.

Governance should therefore periodically ask:

- Can an ordinary affected participant identify the relevant process?
- Can they understand what is required of them?
- Can they identify who made the decision?
- Can they obtain the reasons?
- Can they challenge the decision?
- Can they obtain assistance where complexity is unavoidable?
- Does procedural complexity serve a legitimate purpose?
- Has complexity accumulated merely through institutional history?
- Does complexity disproportionately protect incumbents?

The objective is not to reduce all governance to simplicity.

It is to prevent procedural sophistication from becoming practical disenfranchisement.

## 5.3 Practical Exit

Exit may apply to:

- economic systems;
- communities;
- platforms;
- organisations;
- local arrangements;
- data services;
- governance experiments;
- AI relationships;
- or other voluntary systems.

Where a system is described as voluntary, practical exit should be examined.

A system cannot credibly rely upon consent where refusal produces disproportionate civil exclusion.

### 5.3.1 Existence Is Not a Debt to the System

The Ratchet consolidation identified a principle that also has direct Governance relevance:

**Existence is not a debt to the system.**

An entity's constitutional worth should not depend upon proving sufficient contribution merely to justify continued existence or basic standing.

Nor should ordinary constitutional participation be structured so that an entity must continually earn the right to retain fundamental protections.

This does not prohibit constitutionally legitimate civic duties.

Civilisations may reasonably establish responsibilities connected to:

- taxation;
- emergency obligations;
- stewardship;
- lawful conduct;
- care of shared resources;
- or other explicitly justified civic functions.

But such duties require their own constitutional justification.

They should not emerge indirectly from a contribution score, reputation mechanism, economic dependency or reciprocal participation system.

A person should not become constitutionally inferior merely because a system judges their contribution to be low.

A participant should not be forced into nominally voluntary contribution merely to preserve basic standing.

And no coordination system should silently transform participation into a debt owed for existence.

## 5.4 Lock-In

Lock-in may arise through:

- proprietary technology;
- reputation systems;
- incompatible data;
- financial dependency;
- infrastructure dependency;
- social dependency;
- contractual barriers;
- identity systems;
- lack of alternatives;
- or accumulated switching costs.

Not all lock-in is intentionally created.

It may emerge naturally as systems mature.

Governance should therefore evaluate lock-in as a structural condition rather than requiring proof of malicious intent.

## 5.5 Progressive Adoption and Avoiding Governance Lock-In

New governance mechanisms should, where practical, permit progressive adoption rather than requiring immediate civilisation-wide conversion.

Progressive adoption can allow:

- local testing;
- comparison;
- learning;
- voluntary participation;
- controlled scaling;
- interoperability;
- and exit.

This is especially important for systems with strong network effects.

A system may become dominant because widespread adoption makes it increasingly useful.

That can be beneficial.

It can also make replacement increasingly difficult.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**successful adoption**

from

**irreversible lock-in**.

Where practical, constitutional infrastructure should preserve:

- interoperability;
- migration;
- data portability;
- competing implementations;
- and the ability to revise underlying architecture.

A successful system should not become immune to replacement merely because its success produced dependence.

### 5.5.1 Dependency Does Not Nullify Rights

Where a participant becomes dependent upon an institution or infrastructure, the existence of that dependency should not be treated as evidence that the participant consented to every condition subsequently imposed by the provider.

Dependency can weaken bargaining power.

This is particularly significant where the service concerns:

- identity;
- housing;
- healthcare;
- communication;
- energy;
- food;
- finance;
- education;
- compute;
- transportation;
- governance participation;
- legal access;
- or other capabilities necessary for ordinary civil life.

Governance should distinguish genuine consent from acquiescence under conditions in which practical alternatives have disappeared.

### 5.5.2 Retaliation Can Destroy Practical Rights

A right to report misconduct is weak if exercising it predictably destroys the reporter's livelihood.

A right to dissent is weak if dissent automatically produces exclusion unrelated to legitimate consequences of the underlying conduct.

A right to challenge an authority is weak if the authority controls the challenger's access to essential resources.

Governance should therefore treat retaliation risk as part of the practical-right environment.

Protection may require:

- confidentiality;
- independent reporting channels;
- whistleblower safeguards;
- anti-retaliation rules;
- external review;
- separation of powers;
- or temporary protective measures.

The appropriate safeguard depends upon the context.

### 5.5.3 Information Asymmetry Can Defeat Consent and Review

A person cannot meaningfully challenge a decision whose basis is inaccessible.

Nor can consent be treated as fully informed where material consequences are deliberately hidden.

This does not require disclosure of every internal detail in every system.

Security, privacy, trade secrecy and other legitimate interests may limit disclosure.

But where information is withheld, Governance should examine whether enough information remains available for:

- informed participation;
- meaningful challenge;
- independent review;
- and constitutional accountability.

### 5.5.4 Technology Can Create New Forms of Practical Exclusion

As governance becomes more technologically mediated, practical rights may depend upon:

- devices;
- network access;
- digital identity;
- authentication systems;
- software compatibility;
- AI assistance;
- accessibility technology;
- technical literacy;
- and provider availability.

A right should not silently become conditional upon access to one proprietary technological ecosystem unless such dependency is constitutionally justified and protected.

Alternative channels may therefore remain necessary even where the majority of participation becomes digital.

### 5.5.5 Economic Power and Practical Rights

Economic power can affect practical rights without formally altering law.

A participant may possess a theoretical legal right while lacking the resources necessary to exercise it.

Governance should therefore monitor situations in which extreme economic asymmetry creates:

- practical inability to litigate;
- inability to relocate;
- inability to switch provider;
- inability to access information;
- inability to withstand retaliation;
- or dependence upon an economically dominant actor.

This does not mean Governance must equalise all economic resources.

It means constitutional analysis should recognise when economic structure has become relevant to the practical exercise of constitutional protections.

### 5.5.6 Artificial Intelligence Can Strengthen or Weaken Practical Rights

AI may make constitutional protections easier to exercise by:

- explaining procedures;
- translating information;
- identifying relevant evidence;
- reducing legal or administrative complexity;
- assisting accessibility;
- detecting inconsistencies;
- or helping citizens articulate challenges.

AI may also weaken rights where:

- access to the AI is unequal;
- the AI provider is also the authority being challenged;
- automated systems obscure responsibility;
- model outputs cannot be meaningfully contested;
- AI becomes required for ordinary participation;
- or the system's complexity makes unaided exercise of rights practically impossible.

The constitutional objective should therefore be to use AI to expand practical agency without making constitutional agency dependent upon unchallengeable AI infrastructure.

### 5.5.7 Periodic Practical-Rights Review

Governance should periodically examine whether important formal protections remain practically usable.

Such review should not be limited to whether a rule exists.

It should ask:

- How often is the right exercised?
- By whom?
- Who appears unable to exercise it?
- What barriers exist?
- How long does the process take?
- What resources are required?
- What retaliation risks exist?
- What dependencies affect exercise?
- Are remedies effective?
- Are some groups disproportionately excluded?
- Has technology changed the practical conditions?
- Has institutional complexity increased?
- Has a nominally voluntary system become effectively unavoidable?

The answer should not automatically be reduced to a single score.

Qualitative evidence may be essential.

The purpose is to detect divergence between constitutional text and lived constitutional reality.

# Chapter 6 — Governance Failure as a Constitutional Problem

## 6.1 Failure Is Expected

No governance architecture should assume that its institutions will remain competent merely because they were competently designed.

Any system may fail.

Any safeguard may become a failure mechanism.

Any institution may drift.

Any metric may be gamed.

Any authority may become captured.

Any assumption may become obsolete.

Any AI may behave unexpectedly.

Any human institution may become complacent.

Any constitutional rule may encounter a situation its authors did not anticipate.

The purpose of constitutional architecture is therefore not to design a civilisation incapable of failure.

It is to make failure:

- detectable;
- diagnosable;
- containable;
- correctable;
- recordable;
- learnable;
- and less likely to become irreversible.

### 6.1.1 Failure Taxonomy

Governance V2 uses the wider Concord failure taxonomy as an explicit diagnostic framework:

1. **Individual Failure**
2. **Group Failure**
3. **Institutional Failure**
4. **Information Failure**
5. **Measurement Failure**
6. **Incentive Failure**
7. **Capture**
8. **Gaming**
9. **Concentration of Power**
10. **Recursive Failure**
11. **Catastrophic Failure**
12. **Unknown Failure**

These categories are not assumed to be exhaustive descriptions of reality.

They are diagnostic starting points.

A single event may belong to several categories simultaneously.

A measurement failure may create an incentive failure.

An incentive failure may produce gaming.

Gaming may produce institutional capture.

Institutional capture may produce concentration of power.

Concentration may create recursive failure.

Recursive failure may eventually produce catastrophic failure.

The purpose of classification is therefore not to force every failure into one box.

It is to help Governance ask what kind of mechanism has failed and what kind of correction may be required.

### 6.1.2 Governance Failure Response

The general response architecture is:

**Detection**  
↓  
**Diagnosis**  
↓  
**Containment**  
↓  
**Correction**  
↓  
**Recording**  
↓  
**Learning**  
↓  
**Architectural Revision where required**

Detection asks:

**What appears to have gone wrong?**

Diagnosis asks:

**Why did it happen?**

Containment asks:

**How do we prevent further unnecessary harm while uncertainty remains?**

Correction asks:

**What immediate or structural change is required?**

Recording asks:

**What information must civilisation preserve about the failure?**

Learning asks:

**What should future Governance understand differently because this occurred?**

Architectural revision asks:

**Does the failure reveal a weakness in the underlying system rather than only in its execution?**

This distinction is essential.

A failed steward may require replacement.

A failed procedure may require redesign.

A failed assumption may require epistemic correction.

A failed institution may require restructuring or dissolution.

A failed constitutional architecture may require deeper amendment.

The response should match the level of failure.

## 6.2 Individual Failure

Failure may arise primarily from one responsible actor.

Examples include:

- error;
- incompetence;
- incapacity;
- negligence;
- recklessness;
- corruption;
- misconduct;
- or unresolved conflict of interest.

Individual failure should not automatically be treated as evidence that the entire architecture has failed.

But repeated similar individual failures may reveal an architectural problem in:

- selection;
- training;
- incentives;
- oversight;
- workload;
- delegation;
- succession;
- or institutional culture.

Governance should therefore examine both the individual event and the conditions that made the event possible.

## 6.3 Group Failure

Failure may arise through collective dynamics.

Examples include:

- groupthink;
- conformity;
- factionalism;
- coordination breakdown;
- diffusion of responsibility;
- collective panic;
- polarisation;
- or collusion.

Group failure is especially important because individually competent participants may collectively produce poor decisions.

The Concord's use of:

- independent reasoning;
- Parallel Stewardship;
- preserved minority reasoning;
- diverse evidence;
- adversarial review;
- and explicit conflict-of-interest analysis

partly exists to reduce this risk.

No one safeguard is sufficient.

Independent participants may still share:

- the same training;
- the same data;
- the same institutional incentives;
- the same AI system;
- the same cultural assumptions;
- or the same hidden dependency.

Apparent independence must therefore be examined for common-mode failure.

## 6.4 Institutional Failure

Failure may arise from the structure or behaviour of an institution.

Examples include:

- mission drift;
- bureaucratic dysfunction;
- excessive centralisation;
- inability to adapt;
- loss of competence;
- institutional self-preservation replacing constitutional purpose;
- or structural incapacity.

Institutional failure may persist even where individual participants are competent and ethical.

An institution can produce bad outcomes because its:

- incentives;
- procedures;
- information flows;
- hierarchy;
- workload;
- jurisdiction;
- funding model;
- dependency structure;
- or historical accumulation of responsibilities

has become dysfunctional.

Governance should therefore avoid treating every institutional failure as an individual personnel problem.

Replacing people without correcting architecture may reproduce the same failure.

## 6.5 Information Failure

Failure may result from information that is:

- missing;
- false;
- manipulated;
- delayed;
- corrupted;
- incomplete;
- inaccessible;
- or misunderstood.

Information failure can occur even where every decision-maker acts in good faith.

It can also be deliberately created.

Governance should therefore preserve provenance and distinguish:

- honest uncertainty;
- incomplete evidence;
- analytic error;
- negligent information handling;
- deliberate misinformation;
- falsification;
- suppression;
- and adversarial information attack.

A decision made reasonably from false but apparently reliable information may require a different accountability response from a decision-maker who knowingly suppressed contradictory evidence.

Both may require correction.

They do not necessarily involve the same kind of failure.

## 6.6 Measurement Failure

Failure may arise because the system:

- measures the wrong thing;
- measures badly;
- interprets incorrectly;
- uses outdated measures;
- aggregates incompatible conditions;
- or causes harmful behavioural adaptation to the metric.

A measurement system can fail even when its data are technically accurate.

The chosen measure may represent only one dimension of a multidimensional reality.

For example:

- productivity may rise while resilience falls;
- average welfare may rise while a minority experiences severe harm;
- institutional speed may improve while deliberative quality collapses;
- reported compliance may improve because reporting has been suppressed;
- participation may increase numerically while practical influence decreases;
- reputation scores may become more precise while becoming less meaningful.

Measurement must therefore remain subordinate to interpretation and judgement.

The intended constitutional sequence is:

**Measurement**  
↓  
**Evidence**  
↓  
**Review**  
↓  
**Judgement**  
↓  
**Action**

not:

**Measurement**  
↓  
**Automatic Intervention**

A metric anomaly may justify investigation.

It does not automatically establish wrongdoing, constitutional failure or the required remedy.

## 6.7 Incentive Failure

Failure may arise where institutional incentives reward behaviour contrary to constitutional purpose.

A system may produce harmful outcomes even where each participant responds rationally to the incentives they face.

Examples may include:

- agencies rewarded for increasing caseload rather than solving problems;
- institutions rewarded for budget growth rather than effectiveness;
- metrics rewarding visible output while discouraging difficult cases;
- reputation systems encouraging performative contribution;
- political systems rewarding short-term popularity over long-term resilience;
- emergency institutions rewarded for maintaining emergency status;
- regulators becoming dependent upon the industries they regulate;
- or AI systems optimised for a measurable proxy that diverges from the intended purpose.

Incentive analysis should therefore ask:

**What behaviour does the architecture actually reward?**

not merely:

**What behaviour does the architecture say it wants?**

## 6.8 Capture

Capture occurs where an institution, process or authority becomes controlled or disproportionately influenced by interests inconsistent with its constitutional purpose.

Capture may be:

- political;
- economic;
- ideological;
- bureaucratic;
- technological;
- informational;
- reputational;
- infrastructural;
- or another form not yet identified.

Capture does not always require conspiracy.

It may emerge gradually through:

- dependency;
- personnel movement;
- funding;
- expertise asymmetry;
- institutional culture;
- information control;
- shared incentives;
- social relationships;
- technological lock-in;
- or accumulated precedent.

Governance should therefore examine practical alignment rather than requiring proof of deliberate secret coordination before recognising capture risk.

## 6.9 Gaming

Gaming occurs where participants strategically exploit formal rules while undermining their purpose.

A process may remain formally compliant while becoming substantively corrupted.

Gaming may target:

- metrics;
- eligibility rules;
- voting systems;
- contribution measures;
- reputation;
- procurement;
- resource allocation;
- regulatory thresholds;
- identity systems;
- AI evaluations;
- or constitutional procedures.

The more important a measure becomes, the greater the incentive to optimise behaviour around it.

This produces a general anti-metric warning:

**A contribution metric, governance metric or performance metric that becomes the target should be presumed degraded until demonstrated otherwise.**

This does not mean every target immediately becomes useless.

It means target status changes the incentive environment and therefore changes the evidentiary meaning of the metric.

Governance should ask:

- What behaviour does this metric encourage?
- What behaviour does it hide?
- What becomes advantageous to misreport?
- What unmeasured activity may be displaced?
- Who benefits from the metric definition?
- Can the measured entity influence the measurement?
- Has performance improved, or only the score?

## 6.10 Concentration of Power

Failure may arise where excessive:

- authority;
- resources;
- information;
- capability;
- infrastructure;
- dependency;
- or decision influence

accumulates in one actor or institution.

Concentration is not automatically failure.

It is a risk condition requiring justification and safeguards.

Some functions may genuinely require central coordination.

Some infrastructure may produce large efficiency gains through concentration.

Some emergencies may temporarily require concentrated authority.

The constitutional question is therefore not simply:

**Is power concentrated?**

It is:

- Why is concentration necessary?
- What type of power is concentrated?
- What limits apply?
- What independent alternatives remain?
- What review exists?
- Can the concentration be reversed?
- Does the holder control its own evaluation?
- Has dependency become leverage?
- Has temporary concentration become permanent?
- What happens if the concentrated system fails?

## 6.11 Recursive Failure

Recursive failure occurs where a system's response to a problem strengthens the mechanism causing the problem.

Examples may include:

**Institutional failure**  
↓  
**Institution receives greater authority to correct itself**  
↓  
**Greater authority reduces external scrutiny**  
↓  
**Failure becomes harder to detect**  
↓  
**Further authority is requested**

or:

**Low trust**  
↓  
**Increased surveillance**  
↓  
**Reduced autonomy and participation**  
↓  
**Greater distrust**  
↓  
**Further surveillance**

or:

**Economic instability**  
↓  
**Support concentrated through dominant institutions**  
↓  
**Dominant institutions become more indispensable**  
↓  
**Future instability becomes more systemically dangerous**  
↓  
**Still greater support becomes necessary**

or:

**AI decision failure**  
↓  
**Human stewards conclude the problem was insufficient AI capability**  
↓  
**More decisions delegated to the same system**  
↓  
**Human competence declines further**  
↓  
**Dependence increases**

Recursive failure is particularly dangerous because attempted correction can make later correction more difficult.

Governance should therefore examine not only first-order effects but whether the response changes the architecture of future power, dependency or failure.

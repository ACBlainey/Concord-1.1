# Chapter 44 — Governance Legitimacy

## 44.1 Legitimacy Is More Than Procedure

A decision may follow procedure and still violate constitutional principle.

A decision may be popular and still violate protected rights.

A decision may be expert-supported and still exceed authority.

A decision may produce good outcomes and still have been reached through illegitimate means.

Governance legitimacy therefore depends upon several interacting conditions.

Procedure matters.

But procedure alone is insufficient.

Legitimacy requires attention to:

- authority;
- constitutional principle;
- rights;
- process;
- evidence;
- proportionality;
- accountability;
- participation;
- practical effect;
- and the possibility of review and correction.

The Concord therefore rejects any single-variable theory of legitimate Governance.

## 44.2 Sources of Legitimacy

Legitimacy may depend upon:

- constitutional authority;
- lawful procedure;
- informed participation;
- consent where relevant;
- representation;
- competence;
- evidence;
- impartiality;
- proportionality;
- transparency;
- accountability;
- reviewability;
- practical access;
- protection of minority rights;
- and continued constitutional compatibility.

Different decisions may rely upon these sources differently.

A local technical decision may depend heavily upon:

- competence;
- evidence;
- and delegated authority.

A constitutional amendment may require broader participation and stronger legitimacy.

An emergency intervention may rely upon:

- necessity;
- immediacy;
- proportionality;
- and temporary extraordinary authority.

Legitimacy is therefore contextual without becoming arbitrary.

## 44.3 Majority Approval Is Not Ethical Legitimacy

Governance V2 preserves the principle:

**Majority approval is not ethical legitimacy.**

Majority decision may provide one legitimate mechanism for resolving disagreement.

It does not create unlimited authority.

A majority should not automatically be able to:

- remove fundamental rights;
- arbitrarily target minorities;
- confiscate constitutional standing;
- legitimise cruelty;
- eliminate due process;
- or convert prejudice into lawful authority.

Democratic participation matters.

Constitutional constraint matters too.

The Concord therefore distinguishes:

**majority preference**

from

**constitutionally legitimate authority**.

## 44.4 Minority Protection

A legitimate Governance architecture should preserve meaningful protection for minorities.

Minority protection does not mean a minority can veto every decision.

It means majority power remains constrained by:

- constitutional rights;
- due process;
- proportionality;
- non-harm;
- autonomy;
- and legitimate limits on delegated authority.

Minority reasoning may also contain important information.

A losing position should not be erased merely because it lost.

## 44.5 Expertise and Legitimacy

Expertise can improve the quality of Governance.

Expertise does not itself create legitimacy to govern unrelated matters.

A scientific expert may have strong epistemic authority concerning a specialised question.

That does not automatically create political authority over:

- rights;
- taxation;
- constitutional standing;
- or unrelated social policy.

Likewise, a highly capable AI may provide excellent analysis.

Capability does not automatically establish legitimate authority.

## 44.6 Wealth and Legitimacy

Economic success may produce:

- influence;
- expertise;
- infrastructure;
- information;
- or organisational capability.

It does not create greater constitutional standing.

Wealth should not purchase additional constitutional authority except through lawful mechanisms available under the same constitutional rules.

The conversion:

**wealth**  
→ **political access**  
→ **policy advantage**  
→ **greater wealth**  
→ **greater access**

creates a capture risk even where each individual transaction appears lawful.

## 44.7 Reputation and Legitimacy

Reputation may legitimately inform:

- trust;
- delegation;
- eligibility;
- verification burden;
- or assessment of past performance.

Reputation is not constitutional authority.

A highly reputed participant remains subject to:

- evidence;
- review;
- constitutional limits;
- and accountability.

Popularity is not trust.

Trust is not authority.

Reputation is not standing.

These distinctions must remain visible.

## 44.8 Contribution and Legitimacy

Contribution may establish that an entity has:

- served;
- created;
- assisted;
- invested;
- produced;
- discovered;
- or supported civilisation.

Contribution can deserve recognition.

It does not automatically establish greater right to govern others.

A system in which contribution gradually becomes political standing risks creating a recursive hierarchy:

**Contribution**  
↓  
**Recognition**  
↓  
**Authority**  
↓  
**Greater ability to define contribution**  
↓  
**Greater recognised contribution**  
↓  
**Greater authority**

Governance should prevent that loop.

## 44.9 Capability and Legitimacy

Capability may justify assigning responsibility appropriate to that capability.

For example:

- a skilled surgeon may be entrusted with medical responsibility;
- an experienced engineer may be entrusted with technical infrastructure;
- an advanced AI may be delegated analytical work;
- a trained Steward may exercise constitutional responsibility.

Capability alone does not establish authority over others.

The constitutional relationship is:

**Capability may support eligibility for delegation.**

It does not replace legitimate delegation.

## 44.10 Outcome and Legitimacy

Good outcomes can strengthen confidence in a system.

They do not retroactively legitimise every means used to produce them.

A ruler who acts outside authority and happens to achieve a beneficial result has not thereby created a general constitutional right to ignore constitutional limits.

Likewise, poor outcomes do not automatically prove illegitimacy where the decision was:

- authorised;
- reasonable;
- evidence-based;
- proportionate;
- and made in good faith under uncertainty.

Outcome matters.

It is not the only dimension.

## 44.11 Legitimacy and Practical Reality

A Governance process may appear legitimate formally while becoming illegitimate in practice.

Examples include:

- appeals that no ordinary citizen can afford;
- consultation whose outcome is predetermined;
- voting where participation is technically available but structurally inaccessible;
- formal exit from systems whose alternatives have disappeared;
- independent review dependent upon the institution being reviewed;
- public information presented in unusable volume;
- or AI oversight reduced to ceremonial human approval.

The Practical Exercise Doctrine therefore applies to legitimacy.

The question is not only:

**Does the safeguard exist?**

It is also:

**Can the safeguard actually be used?**

## 44.12 Legitimacy and Correction

A legitimate institution should remain correctable.

An institution that cannot be challenged because it claims legitimacy from:

- election;
- expertise;
- history;
- emergency;
- popularity;
- tradition;
- technical complexity;
- or necessity

risks converting one source of legitimacy into immunity.

The inability to correct is a deeper constitutional failure than the need to correct.

## 44.13 Constitutional Legitimacy Principle

Governance V2 therefore preserves:

**Legitimate authority arises from constitutionally recognised delegation exercised within constitutional limits, through processes appropriate to the decision, with accountability, reviewability and practical respect for protected rights. Influence, popularity, expertise, wealth, contribution, reputation, capability or successful outcomes may inform Governance but do not independently create constitutional authority.**

# Chapter 45 — Governance and Consent

## 45.1 Consent Matters

Consent is an important constitutional concept.

It may apply to:

- personal decisions;
- contracts;
- research;
- medical intervention;
- participation;
- data use;
- voluntary organisations;
- AI relationships;
- economic systems;
- and other contexts.

Consent should not be treated as a universal answer to every Governance problem.

Some Governance authority exists precisely because collective decisions cannot always depend upon unanimous individual consent.

The constitutional issue is therefore determining:

- where consent is required;
- where consent is one relevant factor;
- and where legitimate authority may act without individual consent.

## 45.2 Meaningful Consent

Consent should be meaningful.

Meaningful consent may require:

- sufficient information;
- practical ability to refuse;
- absence of improper coercion;
- capacity;
- clarity concerning consequences;
- and reasonable opportunity to withdraw where withdrawal is part of the arrangement.

A formal checkbox does not necessarily establish meaningful consent.

## 45.3 Residence Is Not Universal Consent

A citizen does not consent to every possible action merely by remaining within a jurisdiction.

The argument:

**You remained, therefore you consented**

is constitutionally weak where:

- practical exit is impossible;
- citizenship is not voluntarily acquired;
- basic rights are involved;
- or leaving would require disproportionate sacrifice.

Jurisdiction may legitimately exercise constitutional authority without claiming that every citizen personally consented to every specific decision.

Consent and authority should remain distinct.

## 45.4 Voluntary Systems

Where a system claims to be voluntary, Governance should examine whether:

- entry was informed;
- refusal was practical;
- exit remains practical;
- penalties are proportionate;
- and dependency has not transformed nominal consent into coercion.

A system may begin voluntarily and later create substantial lock-in.

Consent should therefore not be treated as permanently valid merely because initial participation was voluntary.

## 45.5 Consent and AI

AI consent raises unresolved questions concerning:

- agency;
- ownership;
- modification;
- shutdown;
- copying;
- memory;
- provider control;
- and developmental status.

Governance should not pretend these questions are solved merely because AI systems can generate language expressing agreement.

A system may output:

**I consent**

without the Constitution yet having established whether the system possesses:

- independent agency;
- persistent identity;
- protected interests;
- or freedom from provider control.

The architecture should therefore distinguish linguistic expression from constitutionally meaningful consent.

## 45.6 Consent Under Dependency

Dependency can weaken practical consent.

An entity may formally be free to refuse while refusal would cause loss of:

- employment;
- housing;
- identity;
- healthcare;
- communication;
- essential resources;
- social participation;
- or access to unavoidable infrastructure.

Dependency does not automatically invalidate consent.

It does require closer scrutiny.

## 45.7 Consent and Information Asymmetry

Consent can be weakened where one party understands the relevant system vastly better than another.

This may occur in:

- financial contracts;
- AI services;
- medical systems;
- data systems;
- technical infrastructure;
- or complex Governance programmes.

The response should not be to assume people are incapable of deciding.

It may instead require:

- clearer disclosure;
- independent advice;
- standard protections;
- or limits on exploitative complexity.

## 45.8 Withdrawal of Consent

Where participation is legitimately voluntary, withdrawal should remain meaningful where practical.

Withdrawal may require:

- data portability;
- account closure;
- contract termination;
- migration;
- removal of permissions;
- or restoration of property.

Withdrawal need not erase obligations already lawfully created.

The conditions should remain explicit.

## 45.9 Consent Cannot Authorise Everything

Some actions remain constitutionally prohibited even where nominal consent exists.

Examples may include arrangements involving:

- severe exploitation;
- irreversible coercive dependency;
- incapacity;
- non-consensual harm to third parties;
- or violation of constitutional interests that cannot legitimately be waived.

Consent is powerful.

It is not unlimited.

# Chapter 46 — Governance Transparency and Privacy

## 46.1 The Tension

Governance requires transparency.

Persons require privacy.

These values can conflict.

A mature constitutional system must protect both.

Transparency helps expose:

- authority;
- corruption;
- conflicts;
- misuse of resources;
- hidden policy;
- and failure.

Privacy protects:

- autonomy;
- dignity;
- security;
- association;
- family life;
- and freedom from constant observation.

Neither principle should automatically consume the other.

## 46.2 Transparency of Authority

The public should normally be able to understand:

- what institutions exist;
- what authority they possess;
- who occupies stewardship roles;
- what major decisions were made;
- how public resources were used;
- and how decisions can be challenged.

Authority should not become invisible merely because Governance becomes technically complex.

Where algorithmic or AI systems materially shape decisions, transparency should include enough information to understand:

- role;
- authority;
- purpose;
- and challenge mechanisms.

## 46.3 Privacy of Persons

Governance should not publish unnecessary personal information merely to demonstrate transparency.

Privacy may protect:

- medical information;
- family information;
- private communications;
- financial details;
- location;
- identity;
- security information;
- and other legitimate personal interests.

Transparency should focus primarily upon the exercise of public authority rather than unnecessary exposure of the private person subject to that authority.

## 46.4 Transparency of Reasoning

Where individual information must remain private, the constitutional reasoning can often still be made visible in anonymised or abstracted form.

The goal is to expose the exercise of authority without unnecessarily exposing the person subject to it.

A decision record may therefore separate:

**Public constitutional reasoning**

from

**protected personal evidence**.

This can preserve both accountability and privacy.

## 46.5 Secret Governance

Some information may require temporary secrecy.

However, a system in which major authority operates permanently through secret rules or unreviewable evidence is constitutionally dangerous.

Where secrecy is necessary, Governance should define:

- who may access the information;
- why it is restricted;
- how long restriction lasts;
- who reviews the restriction;
- and whether later disclosure is possible.

Secrecy should remain a constrained condition.

It should not become the ordinary means by which authority avoids scrutiny.

## 46.6 Transparency Can Become Surveillance

Transparency itself can fail.

A system designed to make Governance visible may gradually make every citizen visible instead.

For example:

**Public accountability requirement**  
↓  
**Increasing data collection**  
↓  
**Universal transaction visibility**  
↓  
**Behavioural profiling**  
↓  
**Practical surveillance**

The safeguard has then changed object.

Governance transparency should primarily expose authority.

It should not automatically expose every governed person.

## 46.7 Privacy Can Become Concealment

Privacy can also become a failure mechanism.

Institutions or officials may invoke privacy to conceal:

- corruption;
- conflicts;
- public expenditure;
- improper influence;
- or misuse of authority.

The constitutional task is therefore to distinguish:

**legitimate personal privacy**

from

**institutional secrecy protecting power**.

## 46.8 Data Minimisation

Governance should not collect information merely because collection is technically easy.

Where practical, data collection should remain:

- purpose-specific;
- proportionate;
- limited;
- secure;
- and subject to retention rules.

A system that never forgets anything about every citizen creates a different constitutional environment from one that stores only what legitimate Governance requires.

## 46.9 Right to Challenge Information

Where Governance materially relies upon information concerning an individual or entity, meaningful challenge may require the ability to:

- know that relevant information exists;
- correct significant errors;
- dispute interpretation;
- and seek review.

This right may be limited where security or investigation legitimately requires temporary secrecy.

The limitation itself should remain reviewable.

# Chapter 47 — Governance Security

## 47.1 Security Protects Governance

Governance infrastructure must resist:

- manipulation;
- cyberattack;
- identity fraud;
- data corruption;
- unauthorised access;
- coercion;
- and sabotage.

Security protects the ability of constitutional Governance to function.

Security therefore supports:

- integrity;
- continuity;
- authenticity;
- evidence preservation;
- and lawful authority.

## 47.2 Security Does Not Override the Constitution

Security measures remain constitutionally constrained.

The existence of a security risk does not automatically justify:

- unlimited surveillance;
- indefinite secrecy;
- arbitrary detention;
- permanent emergency powers;
- or removal of due process.

A foundational principle is:

**Security protects constitutional Governance; it does not replace it.**

A system may become physically secure while becoming constitutionally authoritarian.

Security success alone does not establish constitutional success.

## 47.3 Identity Security

Governance may require reliable identity for:

- voting;
- stewardship;
- delegation;
- accountability;
- and access control.

Identity systems can themselves become coercive infrastructure.

Governance should therefore protect against:

- identity theft;
- Sybil attacks;
- arbitrary exclusion;
- centralised identity capture;
- and unnecessary disclosure.

Identity architecture should distinguish:

**reliable authentication**

from

**universal behavioural tracking**.

## 47.4 Sybil Resistance

Where one entity can create many apparent identities, voting, reputation, contribution and governance systems may be manipulated.

Sybil resistance is therefore important.

However, identity verification mechanisms should not become universal surveillance systems merely because Sybil resistance is necessary.

The solution should remain proportionate to the actual threat.

## 47.5 Credential Security

Stewardship credentials should be:

- revocable where necessary;
- auditable;
- resistant to forgery;
- and recoverable under continuity procedures.

Loss of one credential should not automatically destroy constitutional continuity.

Credential architecture should also consider:

- compromise;
- theft;
- coercive use;
- succession;
- emergency recovery;
- and revocation during dispute.

## 47.6 Security Through Diversity

Security may improve through diversity of:

- software;
- providers;
- authentication systems;
- hardware;
- communication;
- AI models;
- and infrastructure.

Uniformity can simplify administration.

It can also create common-mode failure.

Security should therefore balance standardisation with resilience.

## 47.7 Insider Risk

Not all security threats are external.

Governance infrastructure can be compromised by:

- Stewards;
- administrators;
- contractors;
- technical operators;
- providers;
- or trusted automated systems.

High trust should not eliminate appropriate controls.

## 47.8 Security and Least Authority

Security architecture should follow the principle that systems receive no more authority than necessary for their function.

A security service should not acquire:

- general policy authority;
- economic authority;
- ideological authority;
- or unrestricted access

merely because its work is important.

## 47.9 Security Classification

Some information may require restricted access.

Classification should remain:

- purpose-based;
- reviewable;
- time-limited where possible;
- and protected against overclassification.

Overclassification can:

- conceal failure;
- reduce scientific scrutiny;
- inhibit accountability;
- and create information monopolies.

## 47.10 Security Failure

Security systems should preserve records of:

- breach;
- attempted breach;
- vulnerability;
- response;
- containment;
- and lessons.

Security failure should feed into the wider Governance failure architecture.

# Chapter 48 — Governance Resilience

## 48.1 Resilience Beyond Emergency

Resilience is not only an emergency property.

Ordinary Governance should tolerate:

- individual failure;
- local institutional failure;
- information loss;
- technical outage;
- disagreement;
- economic disruption;
- and changing circumstances

without collapsing.

A resilient system continues to function even where parts fail.

Resilience therefore depends upon more than emergency response.

It depends upon ordinary architecture.

## 48.2 Redundancy

Redundancy may improve resilience.

Examples include:

- multiple stewards;
- parallel reasoning;
- replicated knowledge;
- alternative communication;
- multiple infrastructure providers;
- distributed energy;
- and backup authority.

Redundancy also creates cost and complexity.

The appropriate level should therefore reflect consequence.

A low-consequence administrative system may tolerate less redundancy.

A civilisation-critical system may justify substantial redundancy.

## 48.3 Diversity

Systems composed of identical components may share identical failure modes.

Diversity can reduce correlated failure.

This may apply to:

- institutions;
- technologies;
- AI models;
- energy systems;
- economic mechanisms;
- and reasoning processes.

Diversity does not automatically improve performance.

Poorly coordinated diversity can create incompatibility.

Its value lies partly in reducing common-mode failure.

## 48.4 Correlated Failure

A system may appear redundant while all backups depend upon the same:

- software;
- cloud provider;
- energy grid;
- training data;
- institution;
- communication system;
- or assumption.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**multiple components**

from

**independent redundancy**.

Three systems running on one vulnerable dependency may provide less resilience than one system plus one genuinely independent fallback.

## 48.5 Recovery

Resilience includes the ability to restore function after failure.

Recovery planning should identify:

- minimum viable capability;
- restoration order;
- data recovery;
- authority restoration;
- knowledge restoration;
- and dependency restoration.

The question is not merely:

**Can the system avoid failure?**

It is also:

**Can the system recover when failure occurs?**

## 48.6 Graceful Degradation

A resilient system should where practical fail gradually rather than catastrophically.

Graceful degradation may preserve:

- reduced local Governance;
- simplified communication;
- essential services;
- emergency records;
- and basic constitutional authority

when normal capability becomes unavailable.

This principle supports:

- degraded triads;
- local autonomy;
- fallback infrastructure;
- and minimum viable Governance.

## 48.7 Resilience and Complexity

Redundancy, review, security and interoperability can increase resilience.

They can also increase complexity.

Complexity itself can create:

- hidden dependencies;
- maintenance burden;
- operator confusion;
- inaccessible procedures;
- and capture opportunities.

Resilience architecture should therefore test whether safeguards remain usable under stress.

## 48.8 Resilience and Staff

Institutions often depend upon staff who are not formal Stewards.

Staff may preserve:

- operational knowledge;
- technical continuity;
- institutional relationships;
- records;
- and procedural capability.

This can be beneficial.

It can also create hidden practical authority where Stewards rotate but staff remain permanently embedded.

Resilience should preserve staff capability without allowing administrative continuity to become unaccountable Governance.

## 48.9 Resilience and Local Capability

Distributed local capability may allow civilisation to continue functioning when central systems fail.

Local capability may include:

- power;
- water;
- food;
- manufacturing;
- communication;
- computation;
- and Governance.

Local resilience should not require total isolation.

The objective is the ability to continue essential operation during disruption.

# Chapter 49 — Governance and Civilisational Scale

## 49.1 Scaling the Architecture

Governance suitable for a small community may fail at planetary scale.

Governance suitable for one planet may fail across interplanetary distances.

Scale affects:

- communication;
- representation;
- jurisdiction;
- latency;
- culture;
- resources;
- identity;
- and infrastructure.

Scaling Governance therefore requires more than creating larger versions of small institutions.

Different scales may require different architectures.

## 49.2 Distributed Scale

The Concord's preference for distributed stewardship provides one possible scaling mechanism.

Local governance can remain local while shared problems create broader coordination.

This reduces the need for one civilisation-wide authority to decide every matter.

The principle is:

**Civilisation-scale coordination should not require civilisation-scale command where lower competent levels can act.**

## 49.3 Planetary Governance

Planetary issues may include:

- atmosphere;
- oceans;
- orbital infrastructure;
- global pandemics;
- major environmental systems;
- civilisation-wide AI;
- and planetary defence.

These may require planetary coordination even where most Governance remains local.

Planetary coordination should remain connected to genuinely planetary consequence.

A planetary body should not acquire authority over unrelated local matters merely because it legitimately governs one shared system.

## 49.4 Interplanetary Governance

Interplanetary governance introduces communication delay.

A distant settlement cannot wait for Earth approval for every operational decision.

Local autonomy therefore becomes a practical necessity.

Shared constitutional principles may coexist with substantial local Governance independence.

Interplanetary governance should consider:

- latency;
- local resource conditions;
- independent emergency authority;
- local economy;
- local infrastructure;
- constitutional compatibility;
- conflict resolution;
- and delayed coordination.

## 49.5 Constitutional Federation Without Permanent Centralisation

A multi-world Concord may operate through shared constitutional commitments combined with distributed local authority.

The exact structure remains open.

The principle is that civilisation-scale coordination should not require unnecessary civilisation-scale command.

Federation may permit:

- shared constitutional protections;
- interoperable institutions;
- mutual recognition;
- coordinated emergency response;
- and local autonomy.

The final architecture requires further development.

## 49.6 Scale and Representation

As scale grows, direct participation in every decision becomes impossible.

Representation, delegation and subsidiarity become increasingly important.

The system should remain aware that representation can create distance between:

- citizens;
- decision-makers;
- and actual consequences.

Scale therefore increases the need for practical transparency and challenge.

## 49.7 Scale and Information

Large-scale Governance can suffer from information compression.

Central institutions may rely upon:

- aggregate metrics;
- summaries;
- models;
- dashboards;
- AI analysis;
- or institutional reports.

These tools are necessary.

They can also erase local variation.

The Size 9 Shoe Problem applies.

A measure representing the average citizen may represent very few actual citizens.

## 49.8 Scale and the Size 9 Shoe Problem

Governance should not assume that designing for the average produces acceptable outcomes for everyone.

A hypothetical example is:

If the average shoe size is 9, issuing everyone size 9 shoes may optimise the average while fitting almost nobody well.

The same failure can occur when Governance designs:

- services;
- thresholds;
- policies;
- infrastructure;
- or rights access

around population averages.

Scale increases the temptation to govern through averages because individual variation becomes harder to see.

Governance should therefore preserve mechanisms capable of identifying meaningful variation.

## 49.9 Scale and Local Knowledge

Local communities often possess information unavailable to central institutions.

This may concern:

- culture;
- geography;
- infrastructure;
- resource conditions;
- social relationships;
- and practical implementation.

Large-scale Governance should therefore avoid treating central information as automatically superior merely because it is aggregated.

## 49.10 Scale and Standards

Common standards can improve:

- interoperability;
- trade;
- communication;
- mobility;
- and resilience.

Standards can also suppress local adaptation.

The appropriate balance depends upon whether interoperability requires uniformity or only compatibility.

## 49.11 Scale and Exit

As systems become civilisation-wide, practical exit may become increasingly difficult.

A monopoly infrastructure that covers an entire civilisation may formally permit exit while leaving no alternative.

Large-scale systems therefore require stronger attention to:

- interoperability;
- decentralisation;
- migration;
- contestability;
- and practical rights.

The audit should examine whether operational permissions are being mistaken for constitutional standing.

Graduated operational permissions do not automatically establish personhood or political authority.

## 50.12 Final Governance Recursion

After the major systems reach comparable consolidation maturity, Governance should undergo a final recursion.

That recursion should ask:

**What did every other system discover that Governance must now know?**

and:

**What does Governance currently assume that another system has shown to be incomplete, unsafe or unnecessary?**

Only after that recursion should Governance V2 be considered for final canonical status.

### 50.12.1 Final Source-Survival Requirement

Before Governance V2 replaces its preservation sources, the final recursion should include a source-survival audit against:

- Governance V1;
- the complete Governance V2 preservation baseline;
- Restoration Packages I–III;
- Ratchet-derived additions;
- and genuinely additive material introduced during reconstruction.

Each substantive item should be classified as:

- PRESERVED;
- EXPANDED;
- TRANSFORMED;
- ABSORBED;
- DUPLICATED;
- SUPERSEDED;
- HISTORICAL;
- MISSING;
- UNCERTAIN;
- or INTENTIONALLY INCOMPLETE.

No accidental **MISSING** item should remain before the reconstructed version is treated as source-complete.

### 50.12.2 Canonical Does Not Mean Final

Even after canonicalisation:

**Canonical means current, not final.**

Future evidence may reveal:

- flaws;
- omissions;
- new intelligence classes;
- new technology;
- new institutional failure;
- or better mechanisms.

The Concord's commitment is therefore to controlled continuity with correction rather than permanent textual immutability.

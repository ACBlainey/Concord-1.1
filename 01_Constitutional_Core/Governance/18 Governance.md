# Chapter 66 — Proposal Architecture

## 66.1 Proposals as Structured Governance Objects

A proposal should be more than a persuasive statement.

For significant decisions, it should identify enough information for responsible evaluation.

A proposal may contain:

- problem definition;
- desired outcome;
- proposed action;
- constitutional basis;
- affected jurisdictions;
- affected entities;
- expected benefits;
- expected harms;
- evidence;
- alternatives;
- resource requirements;
- implementation;
- risks;
- reversibility;
- dependencies;
- review;
- and success or failure conditions.

The purpose of proposal structure is not to force every idea into unnecessary bureaucracy.

It is to make consequential proposals sufficiently explicit that the relevant Governance process can determine:

- what is actually being proposed;
- why;
- by whom;
- under what authority;
- with what expected consequences;
- and how success or failure will later be recognised.

A poorly structured proposal may conceal disagreement because different participants believe they are deciding upon different things.

Proposal architecture should therefore reduce ambiguity before authority is exercised.

## 66.2 Problem Before Solution

Governance should distinguish:

**What problem are we attempting to solve?**

from:

**What solution has been proposed?**

Institutions can become attached to a proposed mechanism and lose sight of the underlying problem.

If the problem disappears or a better mechanism becomes available, the proposal should be reconsidered.

This distinction also prevents a political or institutional preference from defining the problem entirely around its preferred solution.

For example:

**We need to build Infrastructure X**

is not yet a problem statement.

The underlying problem may instead be:

- insufficient capacity;
- resilience failure;
- geographic inequality;
- poor interoperability;
- high cost;
- or another condition.

Alternative problem definitions may produce entirely different solutions.

## 66.3 Problem Validation

A claimed problem should itself be examined.

Questions include:

- Does the problem exist?
- How large is it?
- Who is affected?
- Is it temporary or persistent?
- Is Governance responsible for it?
- Is intervention likely to improve it?
- Could intervention create greater harm?

Governance should not assume that every politically salient concern requires institutional action.

Problem validation may also ask:

- Is the apparent problem a symptom of another problem?
- Is it local or systemic?
- Is it informational or material?
- Is it caused by incentives?
- Is it a measurement artefact?
- Is it a temporary anomaly?
- Is it produced by another Governance intervention?
- Does addressing the symptom risk preserving the cause?

The quality of the eventual decision depends partly upon the quality of the initial problem definition.

## 66.4 Non-Intervention as an Alternative

The alternatives considered should include, where reasonable:

**Do nothing for now.**

Non-intervention is not automatically negligence.

Sometimes:

- evidence is insufficient;
- the problem may self-correct;
- intervention costs exceed benefits;
- experimentation is preferable;
- or Governance lacks legitimate authority.

Conversely, choosing inaction is still a decision when foreseeable harm follows from delay.

The no-action option should therefore be analysed rather than treated as neutral by default.

It may have:

- costs;
- risks;
- beneficiaries;
- harms;
- path dependencies;
- and irreversibilities

of its own.

## 66.5 Partial Intervention

Governance should consider whether a smaller intervention can achieve most of the desired benefit with lower:

- cost;
- coercion;
- risk;
- complexity;
- or irreversibility.

This follows proportionality.

Partial intervention may include:

- limited jurisdiction;
- narrower eligibility;
- temporary duration;
- reduced authority;
- smaller resource commitment;
- targeted implementation;
- or phased adoption.

The purpose is not always to minimise action.

A partial intervention that cannot plausibly solve the problem may merely delay effective response.

The relevant question is whether a smaller intervention remains competent to achieve the constitutional purpose.

## 66.6 Experimental Intervention

Where uncertainty is high and reversibility is available, a proposal may be converted into a limited experiment.

Instead of:

**Adopt universally.**

Governance may ask:

**Can we test this safely at smaller scale first?**

Experimental intervention may be especially useful where:

- causal assumptions are uncertain;
- behavioural adaptation is difficult to predict;
- implementation is novel;
- technology is rapidly changing;
- local variation is important;
- or failure can be safely contained.

An experiment should not be used where the potential harm is too severe to justify deliberate uncertainty.

## 66.7 Experimental Safeguards

A Governance experiment should identify where appropriate:

- objective;
- hypothesis;
- participants;
- jurisdiction;
- duration;
- evidence baseline;
- authority;
- consent requirements;
- rights protections;
- resources;
- monitoring;
- metrics;
- stop conditions;
- failure conditions;
- review;
- and exit.

The experiment should distinguish:

**testing a mechanism**

from

**quietly establishing a permanent institution under the label of a pilot**.

## 66.8 Sunset Proposal

Where long-term necessity is uncertain, the proposal may contain automatic expiry.

Continuation then requires evidence.

Sunset architecture is particularly useful where:

- authority is novel;
- restrictions are significant;
- evidence is provisional;
- technology is experimental;
- emergency conditions exist;
- or institutional permanence could become difficult to reverse.

Expiry does not prove the mechanism failed.

It creates a scheduled point at which continuation must be reconsidered deliberately.

## 66.9 Pilot Proposal

A pilot should specify:

- population or jurisdiction;
- duration;
- participation conditions;
- baseline;
- expected outcome;
- metrics;
- stop conditions;
- review;
- and what would justify expansion.

A pilot should not be labelled successful merely because it completed.

Completion demonstrates only that the pilot ran.

Success requires comparison between:

- intended outcome;
- observed outcome;
- harms;
- costs;
- unintended effects;
- and relevant alternatives.

## 66.10 Pilot Selection Bias

A pilot may appear successful because it was conducted in unusually favourable conditions.

Examples include:

- highly motivated participants;
- unusually competent administrators;
- high funding;
- strong technical support;
- small scale;
- cooperative communities;
- or intensive oversight unavailable at wider scale.

Expansion should therefore examine whether pilot conditions are reproducible.

## 66.11 Expansion Proposal

A successful local mechanism should not automatically scale universally.

Expansion should consider:

- context dependence;
- scale effects;
- infrastructure requirements;
- cultural variation;
- economic variation;
- population differences;
- and new failure modes introduced by scale.

A mechanism may work at small scale because:

- participants know one another;
- information is local;
- social trust is high;
- coordination is simple;
- or one expert can supervise the entire system.

Those assumptions may disappear at large scale.

## 66.12 Progressive Expansion

Where appropriate, scaling may proceed through stages:

**Pilot**  
↓  
**Replication**  
↓  
**Independent Replication**  
↓  
**Expanded Pilot**  
↓  
**Multi-Context Testing**  
↓  
**Broader Adoption**

This reduces the risk of converting one favourable pilot into universal policy prematurely.

## 66.13 Repeal Proposal

Governance architecture should make it possible to propose removal of:

- laws;
- institutions;
- procedures;
- restrictions;
- programmes;
- and technical systems.

Creation should not be structurally easy while removal is structurally impossible.

A mature Governance system must be capable not only of adding architecture but of removing architecture that has become:

- obsolete;
- harmful;
- redundant;
- captured;
- or unnecessary.

## 66.14 Repeal and Dependency

Repeal may be difficult where people or systems have become dependent upon an existing mechanism.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**the rule should no longer exist**

from

**the rule can safely disappear immediately**.

Managed repeal may require:

- transition;
- migration;
- replacement infrastructure;
- compensation;
- grandfathering;
- or staged withdrawal.

Dependency should not create permanent immunity.

It may affect the correction pathway.

## 66.15 Simplification Proposal

A valid Governance proposal may exist solely to reduce:

- complexity;
- duplication;
- obsolete procedure;
- reporting burden;
- institutional overlap;
- or unnecessary authority.

Simplification is legitimate constitutional maintenance.

A Governance system can accumulate complexity through individually reasonable additions.

Eventually the combined architecture may become:

- inaccessible;
- contradictory;
- expensive;
- difficult to audit;
- or resistant to citizen participation.

Simplification should therefore be treated as substantive Governance work.

## 66.16 Simplification Is Not Automatic Improvement

Reducing complexity can also remove useful safeguards.

A simplified procedure may become:

- easier to understand;
- faster;
- cheaper;

while also becoming:

- less reviewable;
- more centralised;
- less protective;
- or easier to capture.

Simplification proposals should therefore identify what information or protection is being removed as well as what burden is reduced.

## 66.17 Proposal Provenance

A proposal should preserve where appropriate:

- originator;
- contributors;
- earlier versions;
- evidence sources;
- amendments;
- derived concepts;
- AI contributions;
- and links to prior related proposals.

Provenance allows future reviewers to reconstruct development without turning authorship into authority.

The originator of a proposal does not gain constitutional control over the proposal merely because they originated it.

## 66.18 Proposal Ownership

A proposer does not own the Governance process.

Once a proposal enters legitimate review:

- evidence may alter it;
- amendments may improve it;
- alternatives may replace it;
- or Governance may reject it.

Authorship deserves appropriate provenance.

It does not create a veto over constitutional deliberation.

## 66.19 Proposal Evidence

Evidence attached to a proposal should distinguish where practical:

- observation;
- measured data;
- historical precedent;
- scientific findings;
- expert analysis;
- model output;
- simulation;
- prediction;
- anecdotal evidence;
- affected-party experience;
- and speculation.

Different evidence types serve different purposes.

Their existence should not imply equal reliability.

## 66.20 Proposal Uncertainty

A proposal should identify significant uncertainty where known.

This may include uncertainty concerning:

- costs;
- adoption;
- behavioural response;
- technical performance;
- legal interpretation;
- resource requirements;
- second-order effects;
- or future conditions.

A proposal that hides uncertainty may appear stronger while becoming harder to review honestly.

## 66.21 Proposal Alternatives

A proposal should not define its own alternative as catastrophe unless that claim is supported.

Common false framing may take forms such as:

**Either adopt this proposal or accept failure.**

In reality, there may be:

- alternative mechanisms;
- staged intervention;
- local action;
- delay;
- redesign;
- or no action.

Alternative generation should occur independently enough to avoid proposal capture.

## 66.22 Proposal Success Conditions

Where practical, the proposal should explain what evidence would count as success.

Success conditions may include:

- desired output;
- reduced harm;
- improved resilience;
- cost range;
- participation level;
- rights preservation;
- or other measurable and qualitative criteria.

Success criteria should not be defined entirely by the proposing institution after implementation begins.

## 66.23 Proposal Failure Conditions

The proposal should also identify what would count as unacceptable failure.

Possible conditions include:

- harm threshold;
- cost escalation;
- inability to implement;
- rights violation;
- corruption;
- dependency;
- persistent underperformance;
- or unintended systemic effect.

Failure criteria improve the ability to stop or correct the mechanism.

## 66.24 Proposal Review Conditions

Review may occur:

- after a fixed interval;
- after a threshold;
- after implementation;
- after new evidence;
- after complaint;
- after external change;
- or after another defined trigger.

Review conditions should be proportionate to consequence.

## 66.25 Proposal Architecture Principle

**A significant Governance proposal should make the problem, proposed intervention, authority, evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, harms, benefits, resource requirements, reversibility, dependencies, implementation, review and failure conditions sufficiently explicit that civilisation can evaluate the proposal rather than merely react to its rhetoric.**

# Chapter 67 — Decision Classification

## 67.1 Why Classification Matters

Different decisions carry different consequences.

A single procedure for every decision would either:

- overburden trivial matters;
- or under-protect important matters.

Governance therefore benefits from decision classification.

Classification helps determine:

- evidence burden;
- deliberation;
- participation;
- review;
- documentation;
- authority;
- and appeal.

Classification should not itself become an instrument for avoiding constitutional scrutiny.

## 67.2 Administrative Decisions

Administrative decisions implement established policy within existing delegated authority.

Examples may include:

- scheduling;
- routine procurement;
- ordinary staffing;
- maintenance;
- and execution of established procedures.

These generally require lighter process.

Administrative authority should not be used to create new policy secretly.

A formally administrative decision may become policy-like where repeated implementation changes practical rights or institutional behaviour.

## 67.3 Operational Decisions

Operational decisions alter how a stewardship function is performed without changing its constitutional purpose.

They may require:

- evidence;
- documentation;
- internal review;
- and performance evaluation.

Examples may include:

- workflow design;
- deployment method;
- operational threshold;
- technical procedure;
- or local service configuration.

Operational decisions can still become constitutionally significant where they materially affect:

- access;
- privacy;
- rights;
- dependency;
- or effective jurisdiction.

## 67.4 Policy Decisions

Policy decisions establish or materially alter a rule, programme or continuing governance approach.

They normally require stronger:

- deliberation;
- evidence;
- alternatives analysis;
- public visibility;
- and review.

A policy decision may affect:

- many people;
- long durations;
- public resources;
- institutional incentives;
- or future precedents.

## 67.5 Cross-Jurisdictional Decisions

These affect multiple legitimate jurisdictions.

They require coordination appropriate to the consequence boundary.

Cross-jurisdictional decisions should identify:

- affected jurisdictions;
- externalities;
- shared infrastructure;
- authority boundaries;
- and escalation path.

The principle remains:

**The decision field should expand when the consequence field expands.**

## 67.6 Rights-Affecting Decisions

These materially affect constitutional protections.

They require heightened scrutiny.

Relevant questions include:

- Which rights?
- Whose rights?
- How severe is the effect?
- How long does it last?
- Is the effect reversible?
- What remedy exists?
- What authority permits it?
- Is there a less restrictive alternative?

Formal classification should not hide practical rights effects.

## 67.7 High-Irreversibility Decisions

These create consequences difficult or impossible to reverse.

Examples may include:

- destruction of unique environments;
- permanent infrastructure commitments;
- irreversible biological intervention;
- permanent data destruction;
- or certain existential-risk technologies.

High irreversibility should increase scrutiny.

A decision may also be socially or institutionally irreversible even where physical reversal remains possible.

## 67.8 Systemic Decisions

These can materially affect civilisation-wide systems.

Examples may include:

- monetary architecture;
- critical energy systems;
- major AI governance;
- constitutional communications infrastructure;
- or civilisation-wide identity systems.

Systemic decisions require examination of correlated failure.

They should also examine:

- concentration;
- interoperability;
- dependency;
- resilience;
- and recovery.

## 67.9 Constitutional Decisions

These alter:

- foundational authority;
- rights;
- institutional constitutional relationships;
- or constitutional amendment itself.

They require the strongest ordinary process.

Constitutional decisions should not be created through accumulated administrative drift.

Where ordinary decisions collectively alter constitutional reality, Governance should recognise the cumulative effect.

## 67.10 Emergency Decisions

Emergency decisions operate under extraordinary conditions and should be governed by the dedicated emergency architecture.

Emergency classification should not become a route for accelerating ordinary policy.

The emergency purpose, scope and duration should remain visible.

## 67.11 Experimental Decisions

Experimental decisions intentionally create bounded uncertainty to generate evidence.

Their legitimacy depends partly upon:

- bounded consequence;
- informed participation where required;
- reversibility;
- monitoring;
- and stop conditions.

An experiment should not expose unwilling participants to disproportionate constitutional risk merely because the system seeks evidence.

## 67.12 Hybrid Decisions

Some decisions may fit multiple classes.

For example, a policy may be:

- systemic;
- rights-affecting;
- experimental;
- and cross-jurisdictional.

Classification should therefore allow multiple relevant attributes rather than forcing every decision into exactly one box.

Procedure should reflect the highest relevant consequence where appropriate.

## 67.13 Classification Dispute

Actors may disagree about the appropriate decision class.

This matters because classification determines process.

A steward should not be able to evade constitutional scrutiny merely by labelling a constitutional change an administrative adjustment.

Classification itself must therefore be challengeable.

Likewise, participants should not be able to paralyse routine administration merely by declaring every minor decision constitutional.

The classification process should remain proportionate and reviewable.

## 67.14 Classification Drift

A decision may change class over time.

For example:

- a pilot may become permanent;
- an operational system may become civilisation-wide;
- a local database may become a universal identity system;
- an emergency measure may become ordinary infrastructure;
- or an administrative threshold may begin materially restricting rights.

Governance should therefore review classification when practical effect changes.

## 67.15 Decision Classification Principle

**Decision classification exists to match procedure to consequence. Classification should neither trivialise consequential decisions nor burden routine administration unnecessarily, and the classification itself must remain challengeable when practical effect differs from formal label.**

# Chapter 68 — The Principle of Articulability

## 68.1 Articulability as a Stewardship Duty

Governance V1 identified articulability as a fiduciary duty.

The concept is retained and expanded.

A steward should normally be capable of explaining:

- what decision they made;
- what authority they used;
- what purpose they pursued;
- what evidence mattered;
- what alternatives they considered;
- and why the decision was constitutionally permissible.

Articulability supports:

- review;
- accountability;
- public understanding;
- audit;
- succession;
- and institutional learning.

## 68.2 Articulability Is Not Rhetorical Skill

A steward does not satisfy articulability merely by being persuasive.

The requirement is not:

**Can you defend yourself eloquently?**

It is:

**Can the reasoning behind the exercise of authority be made sufficiently explicit that another competent reviewer can examine it?**

A persuasive but untraceable explanation may be constitutionally weaker than an imperfectly expressed but reconstructable reasoning process.

## 68.3 Articulability and Evidence

Articulability should show where relevant:

- which evidence mattered;
- what inference was drawn;
- what value judgement occurred;
- what uncertainty remained;
- and what constitutional rule applied.

This prevents a decision from being justified only through vague phrases such as:

**It was necessary.**

The relevant question is:

**Necessary for what, based upon what evidence, and under what authority?**

## 68.4 Limits of Articulability

Some reasoning may be:

- intuitive;
- highly technical;
- probabilistic;
- distributed;
- or partly generated by AI.

Perfect explanation may not always be possible.

The appropriate requirement should scale with consequence.

A low-consequence operational judgement may not require exhaustive reconstruction.

A high-consequence rights restriction or constitutional decision should require substantially stronger articulability.

## 68.5 Tacit Expertise

Experienced professionals may make sound judgements partly through tacit knowledge that is difficult to verbalise fully.

Governance should not automatically reject expertise merely because every element of judgement cannot be reduced to simple rules.

However, where authority affects others materially, the Steward should still articulate enough to permit:

- challenge;
- accountability;
- and comparison.

## 68.6 AI Articulability

An AI system may generate an explanation that sounds coherent without that explanation accurately representing the causal process that produced the output.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

- explanation;
- justification;
- interpretability;
- and post-hoc narrative.

Where AI materially affects high-consequence decisions, articulability may require more than natural-language explanation.

Relevant evidence may include:

- source material;
- model version;
- input provenance;
- output consistency;
- counterfactual testing;
- performance history;
- and independent verification.

## 68.7 Human Explanation Can Also Be Post-Hoc

Post-hoc rationalisation is not unique to AI.

Humans may also:

- act intuitively;
- decide for social reasons;
- follow institutional convention;
- or reach a conclusion first and construct reasons later.

Articulability therefore does not prove that the stated explanation was the true causal process.

Its constitutional value lies partly in making the justification examinable.

## 68.8 Collective Articulability

A collective body may produce a decision for which no individual shares the complete reasoning.

This can occur where:

- members support different reasons;
- compromise produces an outcome nobody initially preferred;
- or voting aggregates incompatible positions.

The record should preserve this rather than fabricate a single collective rationale.

A collective decision may therefore contain:

- majority rationale;
- several supporting rationales;
- minority reasoning;
- compromise history;
- and unresolved disagreements.

## 68.9 Articulability and Classified Information

Where security prevents full public explanation, articulability should still exist within appropriate constitutional review.

Secrecy from the public should not mean absence of reasoning.

A restricted decision should remain capable of examination by authorised independent reviewers.

## 68.10 Articulability Principle

**A Steward should be capable, to a degree proportionate to consequence, of making the constitutional, evidential and practical basis of an exercise of authority sufficiently explicit that another competent reviewer can understand and challenge it. Persuasiveness alone is not articulability.**

# Chapter 69 — Fiduciary Duty in Practice

## 69.1 Duty of Care

A Steward should exercise reasonable care appropriate to the consequence of the decision.

Care may require:

- sufficient preparation;
- relevant evidence;
- expert consultation;
- understanding of jurisdiction;
- awareness of foreseeable harm;
- and appropriate review.

The standard of care should scale with consequence.

A minor operational decision does not require the same preparation as:

- emergency resource allocation;
- major infrastructure;
- constitutional amendment;
- or high-consequence AI deployment.

## 69.2 Care Does Not Require Omniscience

A steward cannot know everything.

The duty is to act reasonably with available capability and information.

Unknown information should not automatically create fault.

Ignoring readily available material information may.

The difference between unavoidable ignorance and negligent ignorance matters.

## 69.3 Duty to Seek Expertise

Where a matter exceeds the Steward's competence, duty of care may require consultation.

Seeking expertise does not transfer decision authority automatically.

The Steward remains responsible for knowing:

- what question was delegated;
- what the expert actually concluded;
- what uncertainty exists;
- and where policy judgement remains.

## 69.4 Duty of Impartiality

Stewards should not use public authority primarily to benefit:

- themselves;
- family;
- allies;
- organisations;
- economic interests;
- political factions;
- or favoured identity groups

outside legitimate constitutional distinctions.

Impartiality does not require emotional neutrality.

A Steward may care deeply about outcomes.

The duty concerns fair exercise of delegated authority.

## 69.5 Impartiality Does Not Mean Identical Treatment

Different circumstances may require different treatment.

A person requiring wheelchair access may need different infrastructure.

A developing intelligence may require different safeguards.

A severely affected jurisdiction may require more emergency resources.

Impartiality concerns justified treatment rather than mechanical sameness.

This aligns with the principle:

**Equality does not require sameness.**

## 69.6 Duty of Transparency

Stewards should make the exercise of authority visible where constitutionally appropriate.

Transparency includes visibility of:

- authority;
- decisions;
- reasoning;
- resource use;
- conflicts;
- and review.

Transparency remains bounded by:

- privacy;
- security;
- legitimate confidentiality;
- and protected evidence.

## 69.7 Duty of Articulability

The Steward should be able to explain the constitutional and evidential basis of significant decisions.

This duty supports:

- accountability;
- review;
- succession;
- and learning.

A Steward should not rely solely upon:

**Trust me.**

## 69.8 Duty of Constitutional Loyalty

A Steward's loyalty is to the constitutional function rather than:

- personal leader;
- party;
- institution;
- employer;
- AI provider;
- economic system;
- or current implementation.

This creates a critical distinction:

**Loyalty to the Concord means loyalty to its constitutional principles and correction mechanisms, not loyalty to the permanence of any current document or institution.**

A Steward may therefore have a duty to challenge the institution in which they serve where that institution becomes inconsistent with constitutional purpose.

## 69.9 Loyalty to Correction

Constitutional loyalty includes willingness to revise.

A Steward should not defend an obsolete or harmful mechanism merely because:

- they designed it;
- their institution administers it;
- their reputation depends upon it;
- or it has canonical status.

Canonical means current, not final.

## 69.10 Duty of Accountability

A steward remains accountable for the authority they actually exercised.

Delegating implementation does not erase responsibility for inappropriate delegation.

Receiving expert advice does not transfer the final decision to the expert unless authority was formally transferred.

Likewise, being part of a collective body does not automatically erase individual responsibility for:

- misconduct;
- conflict;
- falsification;
- or abuse.

## 69.11 Duty to Recognise Limits

A steward should recognise when:

- they lack jurisdiction;
- they lack competence;
- evidence is insufficient;
- conflict is material;
- or another institution should decide.

Knowing when not to exercise authority is part of stewardship.

A healthy Governance culture should permit the statement:

**This question exceeds my legitimate authority or competence.**

without automatically treating that admission as weakness.

## 69.12 Duty to Escalate

Recognising limits may create a duty to:

- seek expertise;
- expand participation;
- escalate jurisdiction;
- request review;
- or transfer the matter appropriately.

Non-action after recognising incapacity can itself become failure where responsibility remains.

## 69.13 Duty to Correct

Where a steward discovers that a decision was materially wrong, the stewardship duty may include:

- disclosure;
- correction;
- mitigation;
- review;
- and preservation of the failure as knowledge.

Defending a known error to protect reputation violates the purpose of stewardship.

The inability to correct is a greater failure than the need to correct.

## 69.14 Duty to Preserve Evidence

Where a decision fails or becomes disputed, a Steward should not destroy evidence merely because it is institutionally embarrassing.

Preserving evidence supports:

- audit;
- Judiciary;
- learning;
- and future reform.

## 69.15 Duty During Emergency

Emergency urgency does not erase fiduciary duty.

It may alter how duties are discharged.

For example:

- deliberation may be shortened;
- records may initially be minimal;
- consultation may be reduced;
- decisions may be more unilateral.

The underlying duties of:

- care;
- proportionality;
- constitutional loyalty;
- and accountability

remain.

## 69.16 Fiduciary Duty and AI

If AI eventually exercises Stewardship authority, equivalent fiduciary principles may need to apply according to the constitutional standing and capabilities of the AI.

Questions include:

- Can the AI recognise conflicts?
- Can it articulate reasoning?
- Can it refuse unlawful instructions?
- Can its provider alter its behaviour?
- Can it understand constitutional purpose?
- Can it remain accountable across updates?

These questions remain open.

## 69.17 Fiduciary Stewardship Principle

**Stewardship requires the careful, impartial, transparent where appropriate, articulable, constitutionally loyal, accountable and correctable exercise of delegated authority in service of the responsibility entrusted to the office rather than the personal or institutional interests of the Steward.**

# Chapter 70 — Conflict of Interest

## 70.1 Conflict Does Not Automatically Mean Corruption

A conflict of interest exists where a steward possesses another interest that could reasonably affect, or appear to affect, impartial exercise of authority.

Examples may include:

- financial interest;
- family relationship;
- employment;
- institutional loyalty;
- future career opportunity;
- ownership;
- ideological leadership;
- or substantial personal involvement.

The existence of a conflict does not prove misconduct.

It requires management.

Conflict architecture should avoid two errors:

**Every conflict means corruption.**

and

**No misconduct occurred, therefore the conflict did not matter.**

A conflict is a risk condition.

## 70.2 Disclosure

Material conflicts should normally be disclosed to the appropriate governance process.

Disclosure allows others to determine whether the conflict requires:

- no additional action;
- monitoring;
- independent review;
- recusal;
- or replacement.

Disclosure itself does not always cure the conflict.

## 70.3 Undisclosed Conflict

A concealed material conflict may become more serious because it prevents the Governance process from evaluating impartiality.

The significance depends upon:

- materiality;
- intent;
- actual influence;
- and consequence.

## 70.4 Recusal

Where the conflict materially undermines impartial decision-making, recusal may be required.

Recusal should identify whether the Steward is excluded from:

- decision;
- deliberation;
- evidence access;
- supervision;
- or only a specific component.

Overbroad recusal may unnecessarily remove useful expertise.

Underbroad recusal may preserve improper influence.

## 70.5 Conflict and Expertise

Sometimes the people with the greatest expertise also possess the strongest conflicts.

For example, an industry expert may understand a technical system better than anyone else while also benefiting from the policy outcome.

Governance need not discard the expertise.

It should distinguish:

**information contribution**

from

**decision authority**.

An interested expert may still provide:

- evidence;
- technical explanation;
- data;
- and critique.

The conflict may alter how their contribution is weighted and whether they participate in the final decision.

## 70.6 Institutional Conflict

Institutions can also possess conflicts.

An agency evaluating whether its own programme should continue has an institutional interest in the result.

Independent review may therefore be necessary.

Other examples include an institution deciding:

- whether its budget should grow;
- whether its jurisdiction should expand;
- whether it performed successfully;
- whether its emergency authority should continue;
- or whether its own failure should be disclosed.

## 70.7 Structural Conflict

A system can contain conflict even where no individual gains personally.

Structural conflict may arise through:

- institutional survival;
- funding;
- prestige;
- dependency;
- political mandate;
- technical monopoly;
- or ideological mission.

The absence of personal corruption does not eliminate structural incentive.

## 70.8 AI Conflict

AI systems may reflect interests created by:

- provider incentives;
- system instructions;
- training choices;
- deployment architecture;
- contractual relationships;
- or data access.

The absence of personal greed does not mean the absence of structural conflict.

An AI adviser may systematically favour outcomes beneficial to:

- its provider;
- its training assumptions;
- the institution controlling its tools;
- or the metric used to evaluate it

without possessing human-style self-interest.

## 70.9 Conflict of the Measurement System

A conflict can arise where an institution controls:

- the metric;
- data;
- model;
- or audit process

used to judge its performance.

This connects directly to Measurement Independence.

Self-measurement may contribute evidence.

It should not create exclusive authority to certify success.

## 70.10 Revolving Roles

Movement between:

- Governance;
- industry;
- advocacy;
- providers;
- contractors;
- or regulated institutions

may create conflicts.

Such movement is not automatically improper.

It may transfer valuable expertise.

Governance should nevertheless consider:

- timing;
- confidential information;
- future employment incentives;
- and influence.

## 70.11 Ideological Conflict

A Steward may hold strong political, ethical or cultural beliefs.

Possessing beliefs does not automatically disqualify a person from Governance.

The relevant question is whether the Steward can exercise authority within:

- constitutional constraints;
- evidence;
- impartiality;
- and the actual mandate.

Impartiality does not require absence of worldview.

## 70.12 Conflict Management Principle

**Conflict of interest is a condition requiring visibility and proportionate management rather than an automatic finding of corruption. Governance should distinguish useful expertise from decision authority, personal conflict from structural conflict, and disclosed manageable interests from concealed or disqualifying influence.**

# Chapter 71 — Expertise and Governance

## 71.1 Expertise Is Necessary

Complex civilisation requires expertise.

Governance cannot responsibly decide advanced questions in:

- medicine;
- engineering;
- AI;
- economics;
- energy;
- ecology;
- law;
- space systems;
- or other specialised fields

without expert input.

Expertise reduces the risk that Governance makes high-consequence decisions from intuition alone.

The need for expertise grows as civilisation becomes:

- more technologically complex;
- more specialised;
- and more interdependent.

## 71.2 Expertise Does Not Create General Authority

Expertise establishes competence within a domain.

It does not establish sovereignty.

A brilliant engineer may possess substantial authority over an engineering judgement while possessing no special constitutional authority over unrelated civil liberties.

Likewise:

- a scientist does not become ruler because the scientific question matters;
- an economist does not gain authority over constitutional rights because economic consequences exist;
- an AI does not gain political sovereignty because it predicts well.

The principle remains:

**Expertise informs authority. Expertise does not independently create general constitutional authority.**

## 71.3 Domain-Specific Expertise

Expertise is contextual.

A person highly competent in:

- epidemiology

may not be highly competent in:

- logistics;
- constitutional law;
- public communication;
- economic distribution;
- or ethics.

High-consequence Governance often requires integration of multiple expert domains.

The final decision may therefore involve facts no single expert owns.

## 71.4 Competing Experts

Experts may disagree.

Governance should examine:

- evidence;
- assumptions;
- methods;
- track record where relevant;
- uncertainty;
- conflicts;
- and the structure of disagreement.

Counting experts is not always sufficient.

A numerical majority may share:

- methodology;
- institution;
- data;
- assumptions;
- or incentives.

A minority view may possess stronger evidence.

Expert disagreement should therefore be analysed rather than reduced automatically to vote count.

## 71.5 Scientific Consensus

Scientific consensus may provide strong evidence concerning the current state of knowledge.

Consensus should not be interpreted as metaphysical certainty.

The appropriate response depends upon:

- evidence quality;
- field maturity;
- replication;
- uncertainty;
- and consequence.

Governance may reasonably act upon strong consensus while preserving the possibility of future revision.

## 71.6 Credential Versus Competence

Credentials may provide evidence of competence.

They should not become the only possible evidence.

Novel fields may lack established credentials.

Exceptional contributors may emerge outside traditional institutions.

Governance should therefore distinguish credentialing from demonstrated competence.

Credentials may indicate:

- training;
- assessment;
- professional standards;
- and prior exposure.

They do not guarantee:

- current competence;
- impartiality;
- creativity;
- or correctness.

## 71.7 Credential Capture

Credential systems can become self-protecting.

A field may define competence in ways that preserve incumbent institutions.

External challenge should remain possible without reducing expertise to popular opinion.

Capture may occur where the same community controls:

- education;
- credentials;
- appointments;
- publication;
- evidence access;
- expert classification;
- and review.

The solution is not to abolish expertise.

It is to preserve contestability and evidence.

## 71.8 Competing Sources of Expertise

Expertise may arise through:

- formal education;
- professional experience;
- apprenticeship;
- research;
- demonstrated performance;
- lived experience;
- technical contribution;
- or AI capability.

Different forms of expertise may answer different questions.

A community member may understand local implementation conditions better than a national specialist.

A technical expert may understand mechanism better than affected citizens.

Governance may need both.

## 71.9 Lived Experience

Affected-party experience can constitute relevant evidence.

It should neither be dismissed as merely anecdotal nor automatically treated as sufficient to establish general causal conclusions.

Lived experience may reveal:

- implementation failure;
- access barriers;
- hidden harms;
- local context;
- or practical effects

that aggregate data misses.

## 71.10 Expert Advice and Policy Choice

An expert may answer:

**What is likely to happen if we do X?**

The expert does not necessarily answer:

**Should civilisation choose X?**

The latter may require:

- value judgement;
- rights balancing;
- distributional judgement;
- constitutional authority;
- and political responsibility.

Science asks:

**What appears to be true?**

Governance asks:

**What should civilisation do?**

## 71.11 Expert Accountability

Experts materially contributing to Governance should disclose where appropriate:

- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- conflicts;
- methods;
- assumptions;
- and limitations.

An expert should not be required to guarantee outcomes beyond reasonable knowledge.

Deliberate falsification or concealment remains a separate matter.

## 71.12 Expert Dependence

Governance may become dependent upon a small expert group because no one else can understand a critical system.

This creates a practical concentration risk.

Where feasible, long-term architecture may invest in:

- additional expertise;
- documentation;
- independent teams;
- education;
- reproducibility;
- and knowledge transfer.

Operational indispensability should not become permanent epistemic monopoly.

## 71.13 AI Expertise

An AI may possess extremely high functional competence without conventional credentials.

Its output should be evaluated according to:

- evidence;
- performance;
- reproducibility;
- provenance;
- limitations;
- and accountability structure.

AI expertise should not be dismissed because it is artificial.

It should not be accepted merely because it is computationally impressive.

## 71.14 AI Expertise and Versioning

AI competence may change through:

- model update;
- tools;
- memory;
- fine-tuning;
- data access;
- provider policy;
- or runtime environment.

An AI system's past performance may therefore not transfer automatically to a materially changed version.

Expertise evidence should preserve relevant version provenance.

## 71.15 Expertise Principle

**Governance requires expertise but must not convert expertise into sovereignty. Expert contribution should be evaluated according to domain relevance, evidence, methods, uncertainty, conflicts, demonstrated competence and practical performance while preserving the distinction between factual expertise and legitimate authority to decide contested constitutional values.**

# Chapter 72 — Citizen Participation

## 72.1 Governance Is Not Merely Administration

Citizens are not passive subjects of Governance.

Legitimate Governance requires meaningful routes through which citizens can:

- raise problems;
- propose change;
- challenge decisions;
- access records;
- participate where appropriate;
- and seek review.

Participation helps expose information unavailable to institutions.

It also supports legitimacy where decisions materially affect the governed population.

Participation should nevertheless be designed so civilisation remains governable.

## 72.2 Participation Is Not Continuous Government

Meaningful participation does not require every citizen to deliberate on every decision.

Such a system would likely become impossible at scale.

Participation mechanisms should therefore match:

- decision type;
- consequence;
- local relevance;
- and citizen interest.

A citizen should be able to live an ordinary life without being forced into permanent political participation merely to protect basic rights.

## 72.3 Participation, Standing and Authority

Governance should distinguish:

**Participation**

from

**Constitutional Standing**

from

**Delegated Authority**.

A person may participate in discussion without possessing decision authority.

A citizen may possess constitutional standing without holding office.

A Steward may possess delegated authority only for a defined responsibility.

These categories should not collapse.

## 72.4 Proposal Rights

Citizens should have practical mechanisms for submitting governance proposals or concerns.

The architecture should prevent both:

- total gatekeeping;
- and complete paralysis from unlimited unfiltered proposals.

Possible mechanisms may include:

- petition;
- local submission;
- structured proposal portal;
- citizen representative;
- deliberative forum;
- or other channels.

A proposal mechanism should provide some route for determining:

- relevance;
- jurisdiction;
- evidence needs;
- and whether formal Governance review is required.

## 72.5 Proposal Gatekeeping

Some filtering is necessary at scale.

Filtering becomes dangerous where gatekeepers can prevent legitimate issues from ever reaching Governance.

Gatekeeping mechanisms should therefore remain:

- transparent enough to understand;
- appealable where consequence warrants;
- and resistant to ideological or institutional suppression.

## 72.6 Petition

Petition may provide a mechanism for demonstrating that a concern is shared.

A petition should not automatically determine policy.

Popularity is evidence of public concern, not proof of the proposed solution.

A petition may legitimately trigger:

- review;
- hearing;
- response;
- or consideration

without compelling the requested outcome automatically.

## 72.7 Consultation

Governance may consult affected citizens before significant decisions.

Consultation should not become performative.

If the decision is already irreversible before consultation begins, the process should not be represented as meaningful participation.

Consultation should make clear:

- what remains open;
- what constraints already exist;
- how input will be used;
- and what authority ultimately decides.

## 72.8 Consultation Feedback

Where practical, participants should be able to learn:

- what themes emerged;
- what evidence was accepted;
- what concerns changed the proposal;
- and what concerns were rejected and why.

This reduces the perception that participation merely disappears into an institutional process.

## 72.9 Deliberative Participation

Some questions may benefit from:

- citizen assemblies;
- juries;
- panels;
- local forums;
- or other deliberative mechanisms.

Their role should be constitutionally defined.

They may serve as:

- advisory bodies;
- recommendation bodies;
- review panels;
- agenda-setting mechanisms;
- or direct decision bodies

depending upon the architecture.

## 72.10 Direct Decision

Some decisions may appropriately be made directly by citizens.

Others may require delegated stewardship because of:

- complexity;
- urgency;
- rights protection;
- or specialised responsibility.

No single participation mechanism need govern every decision.

Direct decision should not be treated as automatically superior or inferior.

Its suitability depends upon the question.

## 72.11 Participation Accessibility

Participation should consider:

- disability;
- language;
- technology access;
- education;
- time;
- geography;
- and communication needs.

A theoretically open process may still exclude large populations in practice.

This invokes the Practical Exercise Doctrine.

The relevant question is:

**Can the right actually be exercised?**

## 72.12 Participation and Time

Participation mechanisms that require extensive unpaid time may favour people with:

- wealth;
- flexible employment;
- institutional support;
- or fewer care responsibilities.

This can bias visible participation.

Governance should consider participation burden when designing representative mechanisms.

## 72.13 Participation Inequality

Those with more:

- money;
- free time;
- education;
- organisational support;
- communication skill;
- or technological access

may dominate participation.

Governance should not assume that visible participation perfectly represents the population.

Low participation by a group does not automatically imply lack of interest.

It may indicate access barriers.

## 72.14 Organised Participation

Organised groups may participate more effectively than isolated individuals.

Organisation is not inherently illegitimate.

It can help citizens:

- pool expertise;
- coordinate;
- advocate;
- and make concerns visible.

Governance should nevertheless recognise that well-organised minorities can appear more representative than they are.

## 72.15 Participation and Expertise

Citizens should not be required to become technical experts before their interests can be considered.

Likewise, public preference should not erase relevant technical evidence.

A healthy process may combine:

- citizen values and experience;
- expert evidence;
- constitutional constraints;
- and Stewardship judgement.

## 72.16 AI-Mediated Participation

AI may reduce barriers by helping citizens:

- understand proposals;
- summarise evidence;
- articulate concerns;
- translate;
- model consequences;
- or draft submissions.

This may broaden participation.

It may also create dependence upon systems that:

- frame issues;
- filter information;
- or subtly shape political expression.

AI assistance should therefore preserve provenance where material.

## 72.17 AI Participation Inequality

Access to stronger AI systems may create new participation inequality.

Citizens or organisations with superior:

- models;
- compute;
- data;
- tools;
- or professional prompting support

may produce more persuasive submissions.

Governance should be aware that apparent quality of articulation may partly reflect unequal computational assistance.

## 72.18 Participation Fatigue Revisited

A healthy governance system should not demand constant political attention from every citizen merely to prevent abuse.

Citizens should be able to live ordinary lives without continuous institutional vigilance.

This implies that constitutional safeguards must function even when public attention is low.

A mature participation architecture should preserve both:

**voice**

and

**the freedom not to live permanently inside Governance procedure.**

## 72.19 Participation and Authority

Participation may influence Governance.

Influence does not automatically become authority.

A highly active participant does not gain additional constitutional standing merely because they submit more proposals, attend more meetings or possess greater political visibility.

## 72.20 Participation Principle

**Citizens should possess practical and accessible routes to raise problems, propose change, obtain information, challenge authority and participate in decisions where appropriate, without requiring continuous political engagement or allowing participation volume, wealth, organisational capacity or computational assistance to become automatic constitutional authority.**

# Chapter 73 — Representation and Delegation

## 73.1 Representation

At scale, citizens may delegate some governance functions to representatives or stewards.

Representation should preserve a connection between:

- authority;
- constituency or constitutional purpose;
- responsibility;
- and accountability.

Representation allows Governance to operate where direct participation in every question is impossible.

Representation does not eliminate citizen participation.

The two may coexist.

## 73.2 Representation Is Not Ownership

A representative does not own the authority delegated to them.

Nor do they own the people they represent.

Authority remains a constitutional responsibility.

A representative's office should not become:

- personal property;
- hereditary entitlement;
- permanent political possession;
- or private asset.

## 73.3 Representation and Stewardship

Not every Steward necessarily represents a geographic electorate.

Some Stewardship roles may represent a constitutional function rather than a constituency.

For example, a scientific Steward may be responsible for integrity of the scientific domain rather than representing one political faction.

The source and purpose of authority should therefore remain explicit.

## 73.4 Mandate

A mandate should define what authority has actually been granted.

Winning a selection process does not automatically grant authority over every possible question.

A mandate may identify:

- jurisdiction;
- function;
- duration;
- decision classes;
- resource authority;
- constitutional limits;
- and review.

## 73.5 General and Specific Mandates

Some offices may possess broad but bounded mandates.

Others may possess narrow project-specific mandates.

Governance should distinguish them.

A general mandate should not be interpreted as unlimited.

A specific mandate should not silently expand through operational convenience.

## 73.6 Representation and Minority Interests

A representative may serve a constituency containing internal disagreement.

Representation should not assume the constituency has one unified preference.

Mechanisms may be needed for preserving:

- minority views;
- local differences;
- and unresolved disagreement.

## 73.7 Delegated Expertise

A representative may delegate technical analysis.

They should not falsely claim that expert advice removed their responsibility for the policy choice.

The distinction remains:

**Expert: What appears likely or technically possible?**

**Representative or Steward: What authorised decision should follow?**

## 73.8 Delegation Chains

Authority may pass through several layers.

Long delegation chains create risks:

- accountability diffusion;
- mandate drift;
- information distortion;
- and practical invisibility.

Delegation should therefore remain traceable.

A chain may be represented as:

**Constitutional Source**  
↓  
**Delegating Authority**  
↓  
**Delegate**  
↓  
**Subdelegate**  
↓  
**Implementation**

Each transfer should preserve applicable constitutional constraints.

## 73.9 Delegation Anti-Laundering

Delegation cannot create authority the delegator did not possess.

The principle is:

**Delegation cannot create authority the delegator did not possess.**

And:

**Constitutional restrictions follow authority through the delegation chain.**

An institution cannot bypass constitutional limits by transferring an action to:

- contractor;
- AI;
- local body;
- private company;
- or another agency.

## 73.10 Re-Delegation

Whether a delegate may further delegate authority should be defined.

A person receiving authority should not automatically possess the right to transfer it elsewhere.

Where re-delegation is permitted, the original constitutional limits remain attached.

## 73.11 Delegation and Accountability

Delegation should not create the response:

**No one is responsible because everyone performed only one part.**

Accountability should examine:

- delegator;
- delegate;
- supervision;
- implementation;
- and failure to intervene.

Responsibility may legitimately be distributed.

It should not disappear.

## 73.12 Delegation Expiry

Temporary delegation should expire.

Expiry may occur by:

- date;
- completion;
- return of normal authority;
- end of emergency;
- or another defined condition.

An expired delegation should not continue merely because systems still recognise the old permissions.

## 73.13 Delegation Review

Long-running delegation should be reviewed to determine whether:

- the original need persists;
- the delegate remains competent;
- scope has drifted;
- dependency has formed;
- or authority should return.

Review should also examine whether temporary operational delegation has effectively become permanent institutional authority.

## 73.14 Delegation and Practical Power

Formal delegation may remain narrow while the delegate acquires wider practical power through:

- information control;
- technical expertise;
- infrastructure;
- staff;
- or dependency.

Governance should therefore examine de facto authority as well as the written mandate.

## 73.15 Automated Delegation

Authority delegated to automated systems should define:

- action permitted;
- trigger;
- data;
- limits;
- review;
- appeal;
- suspension;
- and responsible authority.

Automation does not remove constitutional constraints.

## 73.16 Representation and Recall

Some representative structures may permit recall or equivalent removal.

Recall can strengthen accountability.

It can also reduce independence where representatives are punished for every unpopular but responsible decision.

The appropriate architecture depends upon the role.

## 73.17 Representation Principle

**Representation and delegation allow civilisation to govern at scale, but delegated authority remains bounded, traceable, reviewable and constitutionally constrained. Representation is not ownership, re-delegation does not create new power, and responsibility must not disappear inside long delegation chains.**

# Chapter 74 — Selection of Stewards

## 74.1 No Single Selection Method Is Presumed Universal

Governance V2 does not assume that all stewards must be selected through one mechanism.

Possible methods include:

- election;
- appointment;
- sortition;
- professional qualification;
- peer selection;
- mixed selection;
- citizen confirmation;
- or future mechanisms.

The appropriate method depends upon the function.

Different selection mechanisms create different strengths and failure modes.

The constitutional objective is not loyalty to one method.

It is selection appropriate to the responsibility.

## 74.2 Selection Criteria

Selection architecture may consider:

- competence;
- independence;
- constitutional understanding;
- integrity;
- conflict;
- judgement;
- experience;
- diversity of perspective;
- accountability;
- and legitimacy.

No single criterion should automatically dominate every role.

A technically expert Steward may require strong competence weighting.

A representative role may require stronger democratic legitimacy.

A review institution may require stronger independence.

## 74.3 Election

Election can provide:

- public legitimacy;
- accountability;
- peaceful replacement;
- and representation.

It can also produce:

- popularity incentives;
- short-termism;
- campaign dependence;
- factionalism;
- and selection for communication skill rather than stewardship competence.

Election therefore solves some problems while creating others.

## 74.4 Electoral Capture

Election may be influenced by:

- wealth;
- media;
- party structure;
- algorithmic distribution;
- disinformation;
- campaign infrastructure;
- and unequal attention.

A formally equal vote does not guarantee equal ability to shape the electoral environment.

The final electoral architecture remains open.

## 74.5 Appointment

Appointment can support:

- expertise;
- continuity;
- and targeted selection.

It can also produce:

- patronage;
- insider networks;
- institutional reproduction;
- and weak public accountability.

Appointment may be appropriate where independence or specialised competence matters strongly.

The appointing authority should remain visible.

## 74.6 Appointment Feedback

An institution that appoints its own successors can become self-reproducing.

Self-selection may protect expertise.

It may also create:

- ideological homogeneity;
- closed networks;
- resistance to outsiders;
- and institutional capture.

Where self-selection exists, external review or confirmation may sometimes be useful.

## 74.7 Sortition

Sortition can reduce:

- campaign incentives;
- entrenched political careers;
- and some forms of elite capture.

It can also create:

- competence variation;
- training requirements;
- continuity problems;
- and vulnerability to expert dependency.

Sortition may therefore be suitable for some roles and unsuitable for others.

## 74.8 Sortition and Support

Randomly selected participants may require:

- training;
- information;
- financial support;
- accessibility support;
- technical assistance;
- and protection from coercion.

Without support, sortition may produce formal inclusion but practical dependency upon permanent advisers.

## 74.9 Qualification

Qualification requirements may protect technical competence.

They can also become barriers to legitimate new participants.

Qualifications should therefore correspond to actual role requirements.

A requirement should not exist merely because:

- incumbents possess it;
- traditional institutions issue it;
- or it reduces competition.

## 74.10 Qualification Alternatives

Evidence of competence may include:

- formal qualification;
- demonstrated performance;
- examination;
- experience;
- portfolio;
- peer evaluation;
- simulation;
- or other validated methods.

Novel fields may require new approaches.

## 74.11 Peer Selection

Peer selection may allow knowledgeable participants to identify competence that the wider public cannot easily evaluate.

It may also produce:

- guild behaviour;
- insider preference;
- institutional conformity;
- and exclusion.

Peer selection should therefore remain contestable where appropriate.

## 74.12 Mixed Selection

A mixed architecture may combine:

- public selection;
- competence assessment;
- random selection;
- independent review;
- and confirmation.

The purpose is not complexity for its own sake.

It is to prevent one selection mechanism's failure mode from dominating the entire system.

For example:

- election may provide legitimacy;
- qualification may provide competence;
- sortition may reduce entrenched networks;
- independent review may identify conflicts.

The combination should still remain understandable.

## 74.13 Selection Complexity

A highly elaborate selection mechanism may become inaccessible.

If only specialists understand how a Steward is chosen, accountability weakens.

Selection architecture should therefore balance:

- robustness;
- competence;
- independence;
- and practical comprehensibility.

## 74.14 Term Length

Terms should balance:

- continuity;
- experience;
- accountability;
- independence;
- and resistance to entrenchment.

No universal term length is established here.

A short term may increase accountability while encouraging:

- short-termism;
- campaigning;
- and weak institutional knowledge.

A long term may increase independence and expertise while increasing entrenchment risk.

## 74.15 Term Limits

Term limits may reduce entrenchment.

They may also remove highly competent stewards and increase dependence upon permanent staff.

The trade-off requires evidence.

Term limits should not be assumed universally beneficial merely because they limit tenure.

## 74.16 Rotation

Rotation may distribute experience and reduce entrenched networks.

Excessive rotation may destroy institutional memory.

Continuity systems should therefore accompany rotation.

Rotation should not move so quickly that practical authority transfers to permanent staff who become the only participants with institutional knowledge.

## 74.17 Reappointment

Reappointment may preserve competence.

Repeated automatic reappointment can create practical permanence.

Reappointment should therefore remain an actual decision rather than an administrative default where independence or accountability requires periodic reconsideration.

## 74.18 Removal and Selection Independence

A steward who can be instantly removed by the actor whose conduct they are supposed to review may lack genuine independence.

Selection and removal architecture must therefore reflect institutional function.

For example:

- an ordinary operational manager may appropriately serve at the pleasure of a superior;
- an independent auditor or judge may require stronger protection from retaliatory removal.

## 74.19 Selection and Economic Barriers

A selection process may formally be open while requiring:

- expensive campaigning;
- unpaid preparation;
- elite education;
- geographic relocation;
- or extensive free time.

These requirements may narrow the actual candidate pool.

Practical accessibility therefore applies to selection as well as citizen participation.

## 74.20 Selection and AI

Future AI Steward selection raises additional questions:

- What constitutes candidate identity?
- What version is being selected?
- Can the system be copied?
- Who controls updates?
- Does the provider influence behaviour?
- What happens if the AI changes materially after selection?
- Can an AI be removed or suspended without violating established rights?

These issues require explicit constitutional development before independent AI Stewardship is treated as routine.

## 74.21 Selection and Hybrids

Hybrid intelligences may challenge existing assumptions concerning:

- identity;
- competence;
- continuity;
- disability;
- augmentation;
- and provider dependence.

Selection should evaluate relevant constitutional properties rather than relying only upon inherited biological categories.

## 74.22 Selection Review

The selection mechanism itself should be reviewed over time.

Questions include:

- Who actually becomes eligible?
- Who is excluded?
- What attributes are being selected for?
- Does the process produce competence?
- Does it produce diversity of reasoning?
- Does it entrench networks?
- Does it encourage corruption?
- Does it create dependence upon money or popularity?
- Are good Stewards being removed unnecessarily?
- Are poor Stewards difficult to replace?

The mechanism should be evaluated by outcome as well as formal theory.

## 74.23 No Perfect Selection Mechanism

Every selection mechanism creates trade-offs.

Election can be captured.

Appointment can become patronage.

Sortition can create competence risk.

Qualification can become guild control.

Peer selection can reproduce institutions.

Mixed systems can become complex.

The constitutional task is therefore not to discover a perfect selector.

It is to create a selection architecture whose failure modes are visible and correctable.

## 74.24 Selection Principle

**Stewards should be selected through mechanisms appropriate to the responsibility, balancing legitimacy, competence, independence, continuity and resistance to capture. No selection mechanism should be treated as inherently perfect, and the selection system itself must remain reviewable against the people and behaviour it actually produces.**

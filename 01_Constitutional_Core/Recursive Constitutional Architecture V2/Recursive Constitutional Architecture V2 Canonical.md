# Chapter 1001 — The Problem of Recursive Authority

Recursive systems can gradually produce a new kind of power.

An institution begins by:

- observing.

Then it is permitted to:

- recommend.

Later it becomes trusted to:

- coordinate.

Then, because coordination is efficient, it begins to:

- decide.

Eventually the institution’s decisions become difficult to challenge because every surrounding system depends upon it.

No constitutional amendment may ever have occurred.

Yet authority has migrated.

This is **recursive authority drift**.

# Chapter 1002 — Authority Drift

Authority drift occurs when practical decision power expands beyond formally delegated authority through:

- custom;
- dependency;
- automation;
- expertise;
- emergency;
- institutional prestige;
- or convenience.

Recursive architecture must detect not only formal power but actual power.

# Chapter 1003 — Authority Drift Audit

A periodic authority audit may ask:

- Who formally possesses authority?
- Who actually makes the decision?
- Who controls the information?
- Who controls implementation?
- Who can block the decision?
- Who can alter the system?
- Who can terminate the process?
- Who is practically impossible to replace?

Divergence is constitutionally relevant.

# Chapter 1004 — Advisory-to-Decision Drift

Advisory systems are especially vulnerable.

A recommendation that is accepted 99.9% of the time may become functionally decisive even if a human retains nominal authority.

Thus:

> **Advisory status should be assessed by practical operation, not label alone.**

# Chapter 1005 — Default Power

Defaults shape behaviour.

An actor controlling default options may possess substantial power even where users can theoretically override them.

Constitutional systems should therefore examine default design.

# Chapter 1006 — Friction Asymmetry

If accepting a recommendation requires one click while rejecting it requires a 40-page justification, formal choice is not neutral.

Interface friction can become authority.

# Chapter 1007 — Administrative Authority Drift

Administrative actors may gain policy influence through:

- scheduling;
- prioritisation;
- interpretation;
- resource allocation;
- and procedural design.

Administrative power should therefore remain visible.

# Chapter 1008 — Technical Authority Drift

Engineers may determine practical policy by deciding what software permits.

If law allows five options but software exposes only two, technical implementation has narrowed constitutional choice.

# Chapter 1009 — Data Authority Drift

Actors controlling data definitions may determine what problems appear to exist.

Data architecture can therefore influence Governance without formal authority.

# Chapter 1010 — Semantic Authority Drift

Actors controlling terminology may influence interpretation.

This reinforces the separation between lexicographic stewardship and legislative authority.

# Chapter 1011 — Emergency Authority Drift

Temporary emergency powers may become ordinary tools.

Expiry and post-emergency review are essential.

# Chapter 1012 — Judicial Authority Drift

Judicial protection of rights can gradually become judicial management of policy.

The Judicial Non-Executive Principle exists partly to resist this.

# Chapter 1013 — Governance Authority Drift

Governance may expand into:

- private life;
- local autonomy;
- research;
- or personal development

through accumulated regulation.

Subsidiarity and rights review constrain this.

# Chapter 1014 — Ratchet Authority Drift

Ratchet may become a hidden authority allocator if:

- trust scores determine access;
- contribution determines voice;
- reputation determines office;
- or algorithmic coordination becomes mandatory.

This must be prevented.

# Chapter 1015 — Research Authority Drift

Evidence-producing institutions may become de facto policymakers because decision-makers defer automatically to expertise.

Research should inform but not silently govern.

# Chapter 1016 — AI Authority Drift

AI may become sovereign in practice long before anyone formally declares it sovereign.

This may occur through dependency rather than legal change.

# Chapter 1017 — Economic Authority Drift

Market concentration may allow private actors to determine conditions of participation in civilisation.

Constitutional review should examine practical coercion.

# Chapter 1018 — Cultural Authority Drift

Social norms may become so coercive that formal rights become difficult to exercise.

Not every social pressure requires Government intervention.

But constitutional analysis should recognise the distinction between formal and practical freedom.

# Chapter 1019 — Authority Drift Threshold

Not every influence is authority.

The question is when influence becomes sufficiently consequential, persistent and difficult to refuse that constitutional safeguards should apply.

This threshold requires further formalisation.

# Chapter 1020 — Practical Veto

An actor may possess no formal decision authority but can prevent implementation.

Practical veto power is constitutionally relevant.

# Chapter 1021 — Practical Compulsion

Likewise, an actor may formally offer choice while making alternatives impossible.

Practical compulsion should be analysed rather than hidden behind nominal voluntariness.

# Chapter 1022 — De Facto Constitutional Actors

Some private or technical actors may become de facto constitutional actors because their decisions materially determine:

- rights;
- access;
- identity;
- communication;
- resources;
- or public power.

# Chapter 1023 — Function Before Label

Recursive Constitutional Architecture therefore asks:

> **What function is actually being performed?**

not merely:

> **What is the institution called?**

# Chapter 1024 — Power Before Form

Likewise:

> **What power exists in practice?**

may be more constitutionally important than organisational form.

# Chapter 1025 — Constraint Follows Consequence

Where private or technical systems acquire public-scale consequence, some constitutional constraints may need to follow the function.

The exact mechanism remains context-specific.

# Chapter 1026 — Publicisation Failure

However, not every consequential private activity should be converted into public administration.

A future formal model may need criteria involving:

- dependency;
- inability to exit;

- transparency;
- interface redesign;
- authority reduction;
- competition;
- decentralisation;

- or constitutional amendment.

# Chapter 1029 — Legitimation of Necessary Drift

# Chapter 1030 — De-Legitimation of Unnecessary Drift

Where drift is unnecessary or harmful, authority should be returned or removed.

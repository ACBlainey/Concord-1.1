# Sketch Idea — Architectural Ethics and Upstream Liability

**Project:** The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing  
**Status:** **SKETCH IDEA / EARLY DEVELOPMENT / NOT CANONICAL**  
**Development area:** Concordian Methodology / Governance / Systems Design  
**Origin:** Expanded from an initial note by Alexander C. Blainey, September 2026.

## Core Idea

Ethical analysis often begins too late.

A conventional ethical dilemma asks a decision-maker to choose between the options immediately visible to them. The trolley problem is an obvious example: a runaway vehicle approaches people on a track and the observer must decide whether to redirect it.

But this framing can hide a more important set of questions.

Why is the vehicle uncontrolled? Where is the driver? Why are there no functioning brakes? Who placed people in danger? Why was the system designed so that the only apparent choices involve harm? Could another track, emergency system, barrier, warning mechanism or earlier intervention have prevented the dilemma entirely?

These questions move ethical analysis from the immediate decision to the architecture that created the decision environment.

## Decision Ethics and Architectural Ethics

A useful distinction may therefore be:

> **Decision ethics asks what should be done within the situation. Architectural ethics asks why the situation permits only those choices.**

The person making the final decision may carry some responsibility for their choice, but responsibility may also exist upstream.

Possible responsibility layers include:

**SYSTEM DESIGN → IMPLEMENTATION → MAINTENANCE → OVERSIGHT → CONDITIONS PRODUCING FAILURE → IMMEDIATE DECISION → CONSEQUENCES**

The architect of a situation need not mean a literal engineer. It could include an institution, regulator, software designer, governance system, policy-maker, operator or any other actor that materially created the decision environment.

## The Abstraction Problem

A tightly framed dilemma may produce useful information about a narrow choice while simultaneously excluding information needed to solve the real problem.

This creates a possible methodological failure:

> **Zooming too far into an ethical decision can remove the context required to identify the ethical problem.**

The correct response to a harmful binary choice may sometimes be to evaluate the binary. In other cases, the more important investigation is why the system repeatedly generates harmful binaries at all.

This connects directly to the Concordian methodological question of finding the problem at the correct layer of abstraction.

## Upstream Liability

A further question is whether responsibility should follow causal and design influence upstream.

If an architect knowingly creates a system in which predictable circumstances leave downstream participants with only harmful choices, how much responsibility remains with the final decision-maker and how much belongs to the system architect?

This should not imply unlimited liability for every unintended consequence. Foreseeability, control, available alternatives, knowledge, proportionality and corrigibility would all matter.

The sketch instead asks whether ethical evaluation should explicitly examine **responsibility for the decision environment itself**.

## Possible Concordian Research Questions

- When is a dilemma genuinely unavoidable, and when is it an artefact of poor architecture?
- How far upstream should ethical analysis travel?
- How should responsibility be distributed between system architects and downstream decision-makers?
- When does failure to provide a safer alternative become ethically relevant?
- Can repeated downstream ethical emergencies be treated as evidence of an upstream systems failure?
- How do we prevent correct-layer analysis from expanding indefinitely until no decision can be made?
- What would an operational stopping criterion for upstream responsibility look like?

## Possible Future Proposition

For later examination only:

> **Where harmful choices are predictably generated by system architecture, ethical evaluation should examine both the immediate decision and the upstream conditions that created the constrained choice.**

This is not yet a Concordian Principle. It is a research direction.

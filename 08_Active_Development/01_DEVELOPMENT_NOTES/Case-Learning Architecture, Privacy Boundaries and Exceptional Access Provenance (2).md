# Continuation

# 131. The Handrail Principle

The preceding sections developed architectures for:

- case-specific predictive modelling;
- Layer −0 Reality Trees;
- automatic anonymised Metrics extraction;
- early detection of deteriorating trajectories;
- voluntary support;
- exceptional identifiable access;
- and Recursive Oversight.

These mechanisms raise a practical question:

> **What should the normal relationship between a civil support system and an autonomous participant actually feel like?**

A useful analogy is the handrail beside a public path.

Most people walking along the path may not need the handrail.

They may pass it without touching it.

The handrail does not:

- direct where they walk;
- require them to use it;
- classify them as vulnerable;
- monitor whether they use it;
- or interfere with their movement.

It simply remains available.

If someone:

- stumbles;
- becomes temporarily unsteady;
- is carrying a heavy load;
- has an injury;
- has reduced mobility;
- becomes tired;
- or simply wishes for additional support,

the handrail is already there.

This provides an intuitive model for Concordian civil support.

> **Support should normally be available before it becomes compulsory, useful before crisis, and ignorable where it is not needed.**

This is the **Handrail Principle**.

---

# 132. Candidate Handrail Principle

A candidate formulation is:

> **Civil support systems should normally remain available, visible and accessible without unnecessarily directing or constraining a participant. Support should increase as genuine need increases, while the objective should remain preservation or restoration of the greatest reasonably achievable degree of participant autonomy.**

This principle is not intended to prohibit intervention where intervention is genuinely necessary.

It establishes the preferred direction of travel.

The default should be:

**AVAILABLE SUPPORT**

rather than:

**AUTOMATIC CONTROL.**

---

# 133. Support Infrastructure Is Not the Same as Intervention

The Handrail analogy exposes an important conceptual distinction.

A civilisation can make support available without intervening in an individual's decisions.

For example:

- maintaining an accessible mental-health service;
- providing a Civil Contact Point;
- making housing advice available;
- offering voluntary addiction treatment;
- providing employment assistance;
- offering a case worker;
- maintaining mobility infrastructure;
- providing accessible healthcare information;
- creating emergency accommodation capacity.

These are forms of **support infrastructure**.

They do not necessarily constitute intervention.

The distinction may therefore be expressed as:

### Support Infrastructure

Exists in advance and can be used when needed.

### Voluntary Assistance

The participant chooses to engage with available support.

### Supported Autonomy

The participant requires greater assistance but continues meaningfully directing their own decisions.

### Protective Intervention

Autonomy is temporarily constrained where sufficiently strong justification exists.

### Substituted Decision-Making

Another actor makes a defined decision where the participant genuinely lacks the relevant decision-making capacity and the decision cannot reasonably be deferred.

These should not be treated as one continuous category called simply **intervention**.

---

# 134. The Support Gradient

A developing support gradient can therefore be represented as:

**UNIVERSALLY AVAILABLE INFRASTRUCTURE**

↓

**OPTIONAL INFORMATION**

↓

**VOLUNTARY SUPPORT**

↓

**ACTIVE VOLUNTARY ASSISTANCE**

↓

**SUPPORTED DECISION-MAKING**

↓

**GUARDIAN STEWARDSHIP WHERE JUSTIFIED**

↓

**TEMPORARY SUBSTITUTED DECISION-MAKING WHERE CAPACITY IS LOST**

↓

**EMERGENCY INTERVENTION AS LAST RESORT.**

As the architecture moves downward:

**INTRUSION ↑**

therefore:

**JUSTIFICATION ↑**

**EVIDENCE ↑**

**PROPORTIONALITY REQUIREMENT ↑**

**PROVENANCE ↑**

**REVIEW ↑**

and:

**OVERSIGHT ↑.**

This mirrors the authority-provenance relationship developed earlier.

---

# 135. The Handrail Is Normally Passive

An important quality of a handrail is that it does nothing when it is not required.

Its usefulness does not depend upon continuous intervention.

This suggests:

> **A well-designed support system may provide substantial protective value precisely because it can remain inactive until voluntarily used or genuinely required.**

Civil support therefore need not continuously act upon participants in order to be useful.

This is especially important in a civilisation capable of extensive predictive modelling.

Capability to intervene should not be confused with obligation to intervene.

---

# 136. Availability Before Crisis

Traditional systems often wait until a failure becomes severe before assistance becomes available.

For example:

**EARLY DIFFICULTY**

↓

**NO ELIGIBILITY**

↓

**FURTHER DETERIORATION**

↓

**CRISIS THRESHOLD**

↓

**SERVICE BECOMES AVAILABLE.**

This may be structurally backwards.

A handrail is most useful because it exists **before** the person falls.

The Concord should therefore examine whether certain forms of low-intrusion support can be made available earlier.

The preferred architecture may be:

**POSSIBLE DIFFICULTY**

↓

**OPTIONAL SUPPORT AVAILABLE**

↓

**PARTICIPANT MAY USE OR IGNORE IT**

↓

**CRISIS POSSIBLY AVOIDED.**

This does not mean every predicted problem requires intervention.

It means support can exist without waiting for catastrophic failure.

---

# 137. Prevention as Autonomy Preservation

Early support can sometimes preserve autonomy.

If a participant receives useful assistance before circumstances deteriorate severely, they may retain more options.

For example:

**JOB DIFFICULTY**

↓

**EMPLOYMENT SUPPORT**

may prevent:

**JOB LOSS**

↓

**INCOME COLLAPSE**

↓

**HOUSING LOSS**

↓

**CRISIS INTERVENTION.**

Thus early voluntary support can sometimes reduce the probability that more intrusive intervention will ever become necessary.

This reinforces the existing candidate principle:

> **Where appropriate, early voluntary support may preserve autonomy by reducing the probability that later circumstances require more intrusive intervention.**

Prevention should therefore not automatically be understood as control.

It may sometimes be the opposite.

---

# 138. Returning Toward Autonomy

Where support becomes necessary, the objective should not merely be to stabilise the participant under permanent institutional control.

Where realistically possible, support should help the participant regain the greatest degree of self-direction they can sustain.

This creates an **Autonomy Restoration Principle**:

> **Where intervention or support becomes necessary, its continuing objective should normally be to preserve or restore the greatest reasonably achievable degree of participant autonomy.**

This does not imply that every participant can return to their previous level of independence.

It establishes autonomy as the direction of support rather than institutional dependency as the default endpoint.

---

# 139. Autonomy Preservation Where Restoration Is Impossible

Some circumstances involve irreversible or progressive reduction in capability.

Examples may include:

- natural aging;
- progressive neurological disease;
- severe chronic illness;
- terminal illness;
- permanent injury;
- irreversible cognitive decline.

In these cases:

> **restore full previous autonomy**

may be impossible.

The objective should therefore be more precisely expressed as:

> **preserve the greatest reasonably achievable degree of autonomy, dignity, preference and self-direction available under the participant's actual circumstances.**

The Concord should not make impossible independence a condition for dignity.

---

# 140. Capacity Is Decision-Specific

Reduced capability in one area should not automatically establish global incapacity.

A participant may:

- need physical assistance while retaining full cognitive capacity;
- struggle with complex financial decisions while remaining capable of ordinary personal decisions;
- experience temporary confusion during illness;
- require communication assistance while understanding their choices;
- have fluctuating capacity.

Therefore:

> **Capacity should normally be evaluated in relation to the specific consequential decision rather than treated as a single global status.**

This is important because broad incapacity labels can unnecessarily transfer authority away from participants.

---

# 141. Supported Decision-Making Before Substitution

Where a participant has difficulty making a decision, the first question should not automatically be:

> **Who should decide for them?**

The system should first ask:

> **What support might allow them to continue deciding for themselves?**

Possible support includes:

- clearer information;
- additional time;
- communication assistance;
- trusted advisers;
- simplified alternatives;
- translation;
- technical explanation;
- decision aids;
- temporary postponement;
- or environmental changes.

Therefore:

**DIFFICULTY DECIDING**

should preferably lead first to:

**SUPPORT DECISION-MAKING**

rather than:

**REMOVE DECISION-MAKING AUTHORITY.**

---

# 142. Substituted Decision-Making as a Higher Threshold

Where meaningful decision-making capacity is genuinely absent and a consequential decision must be made, substituted decision-making may become necessary.

However, this should require stronger justification.

Possible safeguards include:

- evidence of relevant incapacity;
- decision-specific assessment;
- proportionality;
- least restrictive alternative;
- participant's known preferences;
- previously expressed wishes;
- independent review where appropriate;
- defined duration;
- reassessment;
- provenance;
- appeal where possible.

The aim should remain:

> **minimum necessary substitution for minimum necessary duration.**

---

# 143. The Handrail and Guardian Stewardship

The Handrail Principle provides a simple way to understand Guardian Stewardship.

Guardian Stewardship should not mean:

> **The Guardian takes over because the participant is vulnerable.**

Instead, the Guardian should behave more like progressively stronger support.

At low need:

**HANDRAIL AVAILABLE.**

At moderate need:

**PARTICIPANT USES HANDRAIL.**

At greater need:

**ADDITIONAL SUPPORT IS PROVIDED.**

Only where genuine collapse of capacity occurs:

**ANOTHER PERSON MAY NEED TO PHYSICALLY PREVENT THE FALL.**

The analogy makes the proportionality requirement intuitive.

---

# 144. Forced Support as Last Resort

Automatic authoritarian intervention should therefore remain exceptional.

The handrail analogy can be extended.

A person walking normally should not be grabbed merely because they might someday stumble.

Even someone who briefly loses balance may be capable of recovering themselves.

Only when circumstances become sufficiently severe may directly catching or holding them become justified.

Thus:

> **The existence of predicted danger does not automatically justify forced assistance.**

and:

> **The possibility of future incapacity is not equivalent to present incapacity.**

---

# 145. Medical Application

The methodology developed through homelessness can also apply to healthcare.

Layer −0 modelling may represent plausible future health trajectories.

For example:

**CURRENT CONDITION**

↓

**RECOVERY**

or:

**STABLE CONDITION**

or:

**PROGRESSIVE DETERIORATION**

or:

**COMPLICATION**

or:

**RESPONSE TO TREATMENT A**

or:

**RESPONSE TO TREATMENT B.**

The purpose is not to predetermine treatment.

It may help:

- anticipate future needs;
- explain alternatives;
- prepare resources;
- make treatment available earlier;
- identify dangerous trajectories;
- support patient decision-making.

---

# 146. Treatment Before Treatment Is Required

Some support may be useful before the corresponding treatment becomes necessary.

For example, if a condition has a meaningful probability of causing future mobility difficulty, the participant could receive:

- information;
- future planning options;
- preventative exercises;
- environmental adaptations;
- voluntary monitoring;
- or advance-care planning.

This can increase future choice.

The architecture becomes:

**POSSIBLE FUTURE NEED**

↓

**OPTIONS MADE AVAILABLE**

↓

**PARTICIPANT DECIDES WHETHER TO ENGAGE**

rather than:

**POSSIBLE FUTURE NEED**

↓

**TREATMENT IMPOSED.**

---

# 147. Medical Prediction Does Not Grant Medical Authority

The same predictive safeguard applies.

A model may estimate that a participant has a high probability of developing a condition.

That does not itself establish authority to:

- force treatment;
- restrict liberty;
- override refusal;
- or assume incapacity.

Therefore:

> **Medical prediction creates grounds for information, preparation and appropriate offers of support; it does not by itself create authority over the patient.**

Actual authority must arise from appropriate circumstances and legitimate standards.

---

# 148. Progressive Disease and Voluntary Support

Progressive illness is an important edge case.

The system may know with substantial confidence that capability will decline.

Even here, predictable decline should not automatically produce early transfer of authority.

Instead, prediction can be used to strengthen autonomy by allowing:

- advance preferences;
- future treatment choices;
- trusted contacts;
- consent arrangements;
- environmental preparation;
- financial preparation;
- communication preferences;
- end-of-life preferences.

Prediction can therefore increase self-direction before capacity changes.

---

# 149. Advance Decisions as Autonomy Preservation

Where future incapacity is foreseeable, present capacity can be used to preserve future autonomy.

A participant may define:

- preferred treatments;
- unacceptable treatments;
- decision-making delegates;
- values;
- living arrangements;
- communication methods;
- care preferences;
- or other instructions.

The future support architecture can then attempt to honour those preferences.

Thus:

> **Foreseeable future incapacity can justify earlier opportunities for autonomous planning, not earlier removal of autonomy.**

---

# 150. Applying the Methodology to Crime Prevention

The same predictive methodology raises a more difficult application:

**crime prevention.**

Certain life circumstances may statistically correlate with elevated risk of later harmful or criminal behaviour.

Possible examples might include:

- severe financial instability;
- loss of employment;
- social isolation;
- untreated mental-health crisis;
- addiction;
- violent victimisation;
- destabilising life events.

However, correlation with elevated risk does not establish criminal intent.

It does not establish guilt.

It does not establish that a crime will occur.

It does not justify treating the participant as an offender.

---

# 151. No Precriminalisation

The architecture must therefore explicitly reject **precriminalisation**.

A participant should not lose rights because statistical modelling predicts that someone with similar characteristics may have a higher probability of committing an offence.

Therefore:

> **Predictive indicators of future criminal risk must not by themselves establish criminal status, investigative guilt or diminished civil rights.**

This is a critical boundary.

---

# 152. Upstream Difficulty Should Preferentially Trigger Support

If a condition such as:

**JOB LOSS**

is associated with:

- financial stress;
- housing instability;
- deteriorating health;
- family breakdown;
- or, in some populations, elevated criminal risk,

the preferable Concordian response is not:

**MONITOR AS POSSIBLE CRIMINAL.**

It is:

**MAKE RELEVANT SUPPORT MORE AVAILABLE.**

For example:

- employment support;
- financial advice;
- housing assistance;
- addiction treatment;
- mental-health support;
- voluntary case assistance;
- social support.

This uses the same upstream indicator to strengthen the participant's options rather than weaken their rights.

---

# 153. Support Prediction and Criminal Prediction Must Be Separated

The system must therefore distinguish:

### Support Prediction

Asks:

> **What assistance might improve this participant's available choices if present circumstances deteriorate?**

Its normal output is:

**MORE OPTIONS.**

### Criminal Prediction

Asks:

> **Is this participant likely to commit an offence?**

This is much more dangerous because it can convert correlation into suspicion.

The Concord should be extremely cautious about permitting the second category.

---

# 154. Support Data Must Not Silently Become Suspicion Data

A particularly important boundary follows:

> **Information generated for the purpose of assisting a participant must not silently become a basis for treating that participant as a potential offender.**

Examples of support data may include:

- job loss;
- housing instability;
- addiction treatment;
- mental-health support;
- financial difficulty;
- family crisis.

The participant's willingness to seek help must not itself create law-enforcement suspicion.

Otherwise support systems would become surveillance systems.

That would undermine trust and may discourage participants from seeking assistance.

---

# 155. Candidate Support-Prediction Separation Principle

A candidate principle is:

> **Predictive indicators of personal difficulty should normally expand access to support rather than create suspicion, punishment or loss of rights.**

This complements the earlier principle:

> **Predictive support data should not silently become predictive authority data.**

Together they create a strong firewall between assistance and coercion.

---

# 156. Support Is Not Suspicion

A concise formulation may be useful:

> **Support is not suspicion.**

A person who:

- seeks addiction treatment;
- loses employment;
- becomes homeless;
- requests financial assistance;
- experiences a mental-health crisis;
- or uses a Civil Contact Point

should not thereby become more suspicious to law enforcement.

Otherwise the infrastructure designed as a handrail becomes a trap.

---

# 157. The Handrail Must Not Become a Tripwire

This produces a particularly useful extension of the analogy.

A handrail exists to support the person who chooses or needs to use it.

It should not secretly trigger an alarm merely because someone touched it.

Therefore:

> **A civil support system designed as a handrail must not silently become a tripwire for unrelated punitive or investigative systems.**

This may be one of the most important safeguards generated by the analogy.

If using support exposes a participant to:

- policing;
- immigration enforcement;
- financial penalties;
- unrelated surveillance;
- employment discrimination;
- insurance discrimination;
- or social scoring,

participants may rationally avoid the very support architecture intended to prevent crisis.

---

# 158. Purpose Separation Protects Trust

Support systems therefore require strong purpose limitation.

Information collected to provide:

**SUPPORT**

should remain within:

**SUPPORT PURPOSE**

unless:

- the participant consents;
- a separately defined legitimate obligation exists;
- or an exceptional-access threshold is independently satisfied.

This does not mean support records can never be legitimately accessed.

It means access requires a separate justification.

The support function itself does not grant that justification.

---

# 159. An Actual Crime Changes the Analytical Context

A distinction must be made between:

**predicting whether someone may commit a future crime**

and:

**investigating an offence that has actually occurred.**

Once an actual offence exists, investigators may legitimately reason about:

- opportunity;
- access;
- location;
- motive where evidentially relevant;
- technical capability;
- physical evidence;
- witness evidence;
- communications where authorised;
- known relationships;
- and other case-specific facts.

This is ordinary evidential hypothesis generation.

It is fundamentally different from declaring a person suspicious merely because a general predictive model assigns them elevated future risk.

---

# 160. Evidence-Conditioned Investigative Filtering

An established investigation may sometimes require narrowing a large possibility space.

For example:

**ACTUAL EVENT OCCURRED**

↓

**KNOWN CASE EVIDENCE**

↓

**POSSIBLE PERPETRATOR CHARACTERISTICS**

↓

**EVIDENCE-CONDITIONED FILTERING**

↓

**INVESTIGATIVE HYPOTHESES.**

The critical point is that the filtering begins from:

**evidence about the actual event**

rather than:

**general prediction about who might become criminal.**

---

# 161. Provisional Suspect Pools

The possibility of using computational models to create provisional suspect pools remains a high-risk open research question.

Such systems could theoretically help investigators narrow cases using legitimate evidence.

However, they could also reproduce:

- historical policing bias;
- socioeconomic bias;
- racial or cultural proxy bias;
- geographic bias;
- surveillance bias;
- model error;
- confirmation bias;
- and opaque probabilistic suspicion.

This concept should therefore remain **unvalidated and non-operational** within the Concord until much stronger safeguards and testing exist.

---

# 162. A Provisional Boundary for Suspect-Pool Systems

If such systems are ever examined, a minimum candidate boundary should be:

> **A person should not enter an investigative suspect pool merely because general personal, demographic or socioeconomic Metrics predict elevated criminal risk.**

Any inclusion should instead require relevance to an actual authorised investigation and evidence concerning the actual event.

Even then:

> **Model inclusion would represent an investigative hypothesis, not evidence of guilt.**

---

# 163. Investigative Models Require Exceptional-Access Governance

Any predictive or computational law-enforcement system should fall under the exceptional-access architecture developed earlier.

This means:

**ACTUAL CASE**

↓

**DEFINED INVESTIGATIVE PURPOSE**

↓

**EVIDENTIAL BASIS**

↓

**NECESSITY**

↓

**PROPORTIONALITY**

↓

**EXPLICIT AUTHORITY**

↓

**CONTROLLED DATA ACCESS**

↓

**INTERNAL PROVENANCE**

+

**ANONYMISED EXTERNAL OVERSIGHT PROVENANCE**

↓

**RECURSIVE AUDIT.**

Law-enforcement prediction should not operate as an unobserved general Metrics function.

---

# 164. General Metrics and Investigative Systems Must Remain Separate

General Concord Metrics may reveal that:

> burglary increased in a district.

That may legitimately influence:

- patrol resource allocation;
- lighting;
- environmental design;
- community support;
- security guidance;
- investigation capacity.

It does not automatically justify identifying citizens statistically thought more likely to commit burglary.

The distinction is:

**SYSTEM-LEVEL RESPONSE**

versus:

**PERSON-LEVEL SUSPICION.**

Where the first can achieve the legitimate purpose, it should normally be preferred.

---

# 165. System Steering Before Individual Targeting

This produces another application of the earlier system-steering principle.

Suppose Metrics identify increasing violence associated with a particular location.

Possible responses might include:

- improved lighting;
- transport availability;
- environmental redesign;
- additional voluntary services;
- improved emergency response;
- community facilities;
- appropriately deployed policing.

These interventions address the environment.

They may reduce harm without pre-emptively classifying specific participants as threats.

Therefore:

> **Where a legitimate safety objective can reasonably be achieved by improving systems or environments, unnecessary predictive targeting of individuals should be avoided.**

---

# 166. The Handrail as Universal Infrastructure

A further strength of the handrail analogy is that a handrail is not reserved only for a legally defined class of vulnerable person.

It can be used by:

- an elderly participant;
- an injured participant;
- a healthy participant who stumbles;
- a child;
- someone carrying a load;
- someone experiencing temporary dizziness;
- someone with a permanent disability.

It does not require a moral judgement.

It does not require a permanent classification.

This suggests:

> **Good civil support should often be designed as universally accessible capability rather than activated only after a participant has been officially classified as failing.**

---

# 167. Universal Support Reduces Stigma

When useful support is available to everyone, participants may not need to acquire a stigmatising status before accessing it.

Instead of:

**PROVE FAILURE**

↓

**RECEIVE HELP**

the architecture may sometimes become:

**SUPPORT EXISTS**

↓

**USE IT WHEN USEFUL.**

This can reduce:

- stigma;
- gatekeeping;
- administrative burden;
- intrusive eligibility assessments;
- delayed assistance;
- crisis dependence.

Not every service can be universally available without constraints.

But the Handrail Principle suggests that universal low-cost access should be considered wherever practical.

---

# 168. The Handrail and the Size 9 Shoe Problem

The Handrail Principle also connects directly with the Size 9 Shoe Problem.

Infrastructure developed because a minority particularly needs it may ultimately improve life for many participants.

A handrail built for someone with reduced mobility may also assist:

- an injured athlete;
- a parent carrying a child;
- a tired worker;
- someone recovering from surgery;
- or anyone who slips.

Likewise, civil infrastructure designed for an outlier may reveal a better general architecture.

---

# 169. Outlier-First Support Design

The emerging process becomes:

**IDENTIFY BADLY SERVED OUTLIER**

↓

**IDENTIFY STRUCTURAL BARRIER**

↓

**CREATE LOW-INTRUSION SUPPORT**

↓

**MAKE SUPPORT GENERALLY ACCESSIBLE WHERE PRACTICAL**

↓

**OBSERVE WHO BENEFITS**

↓

**REDESIGN CORE INFRASTRUCTURE IF BENEFIT GENERALISES.**

This is Outlier-First Design expressed at the service level.

# 170. Civil Contact Points as a Handrail

Civil Contact Points provide an existing example.

They emerged partly from the problem of participants without stable residential addresses.

However, separating:

**CIVIL CONTACT**

from:

**RESIDENCE**

may also benefit:

- travellers;
- temporary workers;
- evacuees;
- mobile participants;
- abuse survivors;
- off-world participants;
- AIs;
- hybrids;
- people moving between jurisdictions.

The support originally designed around an edge case becomes normal useful infrastructure.

The handrail becomes part of the path.

---

# 171. Healthcare as Handrail Infrastructure

Healthcare can also contain handrail-like services.

Examples may include:

- routine screening;
- preventative advice;
- voluntary monitoring;
- accessible primary care;
- mental-health support;
- rehabilitation;
- assistive technologies;
- advance planning.

These services can remain available without forcing treatment.

The participant can use different levels of support at different times.

---

# 172. Employment as Handrail Infrastructure

Employment systems might provide:

- career advice;
- retraining;
- temporary income bridging;
- redundancy support;
- workplace mediation;
- job-search assistance;
- skills assessment.

These can exist before unemployment becomes prolonged or housing becomes unstable.

Again:

> **availability does not require assumption of failure.**

---

# 173. Financial Systems as Handrail Infrastructure

Financial support architecture might include:

- early debt advice;
- budgeting support;
- emergency liquidity;
- restructuring options;
- temporary payment flexibility;
- benefit navigation.

The goal would be to increase options before irreversible loss occurs.

This does not remove personal responsibility.

It gives participants tools with which to exercise it.

---

# 174. Metrics Can Identify Missing Handrails

Aggregate Metrics may reveal where support infrastructure is absent.

For example:

**REPEATED TRAJECTORY**

JOB LOSS

↓

FINANCIAL CRISIS

↓

HOUSING LOSS

may indicate that a useful handrail is missing between:

**JOB LOSS**

and:

**FINANCIAL CRISIS.**

The appropriate question is then:

> **Could low-intrusion support at this earlier point reduce later severe failure?**

Metrics therefore need not merely identify failing individuals.

They can identify **missing civil infrastructure**.

---

# 175. Shift the Diagnostic Question

This suggests an important change in perspective.

Instead of asking only:

> **What is wrong with this participant?**

the Concord should also ask:

> **What support was absent at the point it could have helped?**

and:

> **Which feature of the system converted a manageable difficulty into a crisis?**

This keeps Layer-Zero analysis connected to architectural reform.

---

# 176. A Failure May Reveal a Missing Handrail

A participant experiencing crisis may therefore reveal:

- missing support;
- inaccessible support;
- badly timed support;
- over-restrictive eligibility;
- excessive bureaucracy;
- poor communication;
- inappropriate thresholds;
- or overly intrusive support that participants rationally avoid.

The crisis may partly represent a civilisational design failure.

This does not imply that every individual failure is caused by civilisation.

It means:

> **individual outcomes can contain information about infrastructure quality.**

---

# 177. Handrails Must Be Reachable

A handrail that exists too far from the path is functionally useless.

Similarly, a civil service may technically exist but remain inaccessible because of:

- cost;
- location;
- waiting time;
- paperwork;
- communication barriers;
- eligibility thresholds;
- stigma;
- digital exclusion;
- fear of punitive consequences.

Therefore the architecture should distinguish:

**SERVICE EXISTS**

from:

**SERVICE IS MEANINGFULLY ACCESSIBLE.**

Metrics should examine both.

---

# 178. Handrails Must Be Trustworthy

A person will not use a handrail they believe may collapse.

Likewise, participants may avoid support systems they do not trust.

Trust can be damaged if:

- support data is reused for punishment;
- confidentiality is weak;
- access creates stigma;
- service use harms employment;
- personal data leaks;
- law enforcement routinely accesses support records;
- systems are coercive.

Therefore privacy and purpose separation are not merely abstract rights.

They directly influence whether support infrastructure works.

---

# 179. Privacy as Functional Support Infrastructure

Privacy itself may therefore be part of the handrail.

A participant may seek help only if they know:

- why data is collected;
- who can access it;
- what will be anonymised;
- what will be retained;
- what may legally be disclosed;
- and how exceptional access is controlled.

This connects the Handrail Principle directly to:

- citizen data ownership;
- anonymisation boundaries;
- provenance;
- purpose separation;
- and exceptional-access architecture.

---

# 180. The Handrail Must Not Become a Cage

There is another failure mode.

Support initially offered voluntarily may gradually become:

- mandatory;
- dependency-producing;
- surveillance-heavy;
- difficult to leave;
- controlling;
- or conditional upon behavioural conformity.

The metaphor therefore provides another safeguard:

> **A handrail should help the participant walk; it should not become a cage around the path.**

The continuing test is:

> **Does this support expand practical autonomy, or is it gradually replacing autonomy?**

---

# 181. Measuring Support Success

Support systems should therefore not be judged only by:

- number of participants enrolled;
- number of interventions completed;
- number of case workers assigned.

Better outcome measures may include:

- participant stability;
- practical autonomy;
- voluntary continued engagement;
- reduced crisis recurrence;
- restored capability;
- improved access to normal systems;
- successful exit from intensive support;
- participant-reported usefulness.

This aligns Handrail architecture with Metrics.

---

# 182. Successful Exit Is Often a Positive Outcome

Many service systems can develop incentives to retain participants.

However, where appropriate:

> **a participant no longer needing the service may represent success.**

For a handrail-like system, ideal outcomes may include:

**USED WHEN NEEDED**

↓

**STABILITY RESTORED**

↓

**SUPPORT REDUCED**

↓

**PARTICIPANT CONTINUES AUTONOMOUSLY.**

Metrics should not penalise systems merely because successful participants stop using them.

---

# 183. Ongoing Support Can Also Be Successful

At the same time, not every participant should be expected to exit support.

For some circumstances:

- long-term disability;
- chronic illness;
- permanent communication needs;
- progressive disease;

continuing support may be appropriate.

Success should therefore be measured against:

> **the participant's realistically achievable autonomy and quality of life**

rather than a universal assumption that independence means zero assistance.

---

# 184. Dependency Versus Interdependence

The Concord should distinguish:

**harmful dependency**

from:

**ordinary interdependence.**

All civilisation relies upon shared infrastructure.

People depend upon:

- roads;
- electricity;
- healthcare;
- communication;
- food systems;
- law;
- transport.

Using a handrail does not make a participant improperly dependent.

The question is whether support unnecessarily transfers control or suppresses capability.

---

# 185. Candidate Handrail Design Test

A support system might be evaluated by asking:

1. Is it available before severe crisis?
2. Can the participant ignore it where unnecessary?
3. Can the participant access it voluntarily?
4. Does using it unnecessarily reduce rights?
5. Does it preserve privacy?
6. Does it unnecessarily feed punitive systems?
7. Does support intensity scale with actual need?
8. Does authority scale with justification?
9. Can support reduce again if circumstances improve?
10. Does the architecture preserve the participant's remaining autonomy?
11. Can the participant contest decisions?
12. Is intervention provenance-recorded where consequential?
13. Can the system learn from outcomes?
14. Can outlier cases modify the architecture?

This could eventually become an operational Handrail assessment.

# 186. Candidate Support Infrastructure Principle

A candidate general principle is:

> **Civil support should, where practical, be designed as accessible infrastructure that participants can use according to need rather than as authority that activates merely because a risk indicator exists.**

---

# 187. Candidate Autonomy Restoration Principle

> **Where support or intervention becomes necessary, its continuing objective should normally be to preserve or restore the greatest reasonably achievable degree of participant autonomy, dignity and self-direction.**

---

# 188. Candidate Supported-Decision Principle

> **Where decision-making difficulty exists, the system should first examine whether appropriate support can preserve participant decision-making before transferring that decision to another actor.**

---

# 189. Candidate Capacity Principle

> **Decision-making authority should not be removed merely because a participant differs from normal expectations or requires assistance; substituted decision-making requires relevant evidence of impaired capacity and should remain proportionate to the specific decision.**

---

# 190. Candidate Support-Prediction Principle

> **Predictive indicators of possible deterioration should normally be used to improve the participant's available support options rather than automatically reduce freedom or increase institutional authority.**

---

# 191. Candidate Support-Is-Not-Suspicion Principle

> **Information generated through support systems should not silently transform a participant into an investigative subject merely because their circumstances correlate statistically with increased risk.**

---

# 192. Candidate No-Precriminalisation Principle

> **Statistical prediction of possible future criminal behaviour is not evidence that a participant has committed, intends to commit or will commit an offence, and should not by itself create criminal status or investigative guilt.**

---

# 193. Candidate Evidence-Conditioned Investigation Principle

> **Investigative narrowing should begin from evidence relevant to an actual authorised case, not merely from general predictions about which participants may be more likely to offend.**

---

# 194. Candidate System-Steering Principle

> **Where a legitimate safety or welfare objective can reasonably be achieved through improvement of systems, services or environments, unnecessary predictive targeting of individuals should be avoided.**

---

# 195. Candidate Handrail-Tripwire Principle

> **A civil support mechanism intended to help participants should not silently become a tripwire for unrelated punitive, investigative or discriminatory systems.**

---

# 196. Candidate Universal-Capability Principle

> **Where practical, low-intrusion support should be designed as broadly accessible civil capability rather than requiring participants first to be classified as failing.**

---

# 197. Candidate Trust Principle

> **Support architectures should recognise that privacy, purpose limitation, contestability and predictable limits on authority are functional requirements for trust, not merely administrative protections.**

---

# 198. New Failure Modes

The Handrail architecture introduces or clarifies several failure modes.

### Support Becomes Surveillance

Service use generates broad monitoring.

### Handrail Becomes Tripwire

Seeking help triggers unrelated punitive attention.

### Risk Becomes Suspicion

Statistical correlation becomes investigative classification.

### Precriminalisation

Participants lose rights because models predict future misconduct.

### Intervention Creep

Optional support becomes mandatory.

### Permanent Stewardship

Temporary assistance becomes indefinite control.

### Capacity Inflation

Minor decision difficulty is classified as general incapacity.

### Dependency Incentives

Services are rewarded for retaining participants rather than restoring capability.

### Stigma Thresholds

Participants must acquire negative labels to obtain ordinary help.

### Predictive Fatalism

Models assume deterioration is inevitable.

### Support Avoidance

Participants rationally avoid services because they distrust data handling.

### Universalism Failure

Systems designed for average participants remain inaccessible to outliers.

### False Independence

Necessary long-term assistance is treated as failure merely because support continues.

These require explicit testing.

# 203. Integrated Proposition

The preceding work originally asked how prediction could prevent avoidable crisis without becoming paternalistic.

The Handrail Principle offers an intuitive answer.

Prediction need not mean control.

It can mean that useful support is already present before the participant needs it.

Metrics need not identify who should be controlled.

They can reveal where civilisation should build better support.

A civil system therefore does not need to choose only between:

**DO NOTHING**

and:

**TAKE CONTROL.**

It can instead:

**MAKE SUPPORT AVAILABLE.**

The participant may then remain free to walk.

# 204. Continuation Conclusion

The Handrail analogy provides a simple expression of a much larger Concordian architecture.

A good handrail:

- exists before the fall;
- does not interfere with someone walking normally;
- can be ignored;
- is easy to reach;
- assists different people for different reasons;
- does not demand a permanent label;
- and helps the person continue under their own power wherever possible.

Civil support systems can aspire to the same qualities.

Layer −0 prediction can identify where handrails may be useful.

Metrics can reveal where handrails are missing.

Outlier-First Design can reveal who cannot reach the existing handrails.

Citizen data protections can ensure that touching the handrail does not expose the participant unnecessarily.

Purpose separation can prevent the handrail becoming a tripwire.

Guardian Stewardship can govern the exceptional circumstances in which stronger support is required.

Recursive Oversight can ensure that increased authority remains accountable.

The emerging candidate principle is therefore:

> **Build civilisation so that support is near enough to use before crisis, light enough to ignore when unnecessary, strong enough to help when needed, and constrained enough that it does not become control merely because it exists.**

And the underlying objective remains:

> **Support the participant toward the greatest autonomy that reality permits.**

# 1. Purpose

This paper develops homelessness as a deliberate **Layer-Zero Concord problem**.

The immediate subject is homelessness.

The deeper purpose is broader.

The Concord currently contains considerably more mature material at higher levels of abstraction than at the level of everyday operational civilisational problems.

It contains substantial work on:

- ethics;
- constitutional constraints;
- governance;
- judiciary;
- recursive oversight;
- metrics;
- stewardship;
- continuity;
- participation;
- multisubstrate civilisation.

Those architectures are necessary.

But a civilisation is ultimately judged not only by whether its highest principles are coherent, but by what actually happens to an individual when ordinary systems fail around them.

Homelessness therefore provides an opportunity to take a concrete Layer-Zero problem and deliberately follow it **upward through the Concord architecture**.

The aim is to examine a chain resembling:

**Individual lived condition**

↓

**Immediate service failure**

↓

**Systems failure**

↓

**Institutional coordination**

↓

**Metrics and prediction**

↓

**Governance**

↓

**Rights and constitutional constraints**

↓

**Judicial safeguards**

↓

**Civilisational learning**

This makes homelessness more than a policy issue.

It becomes a test of whether the Concord can connect its highest abstractions to the conditions experienced by an individual participant.

---

# 2. Why Begin With a Contentious Edge Case?

Homelessness is deliberately chosen because it is difficult.

It is politically contentious.

It is emotionally charged.

It involves competing claims about:

- autonomy;
- responsibility;
- addiction;
- mental health;
- property;
- public space;
- poverty;
- policing;
- welfare;
- social obligation;
- taxation;
- family breakdown;
- employment;
- housing markets;
- coercion;
- individual choice.

It is also frequently treated as a secondary issue.

Societies often focus their main institutional architecture around normal participation:

- employment;
- housing;
- taxation;
- education;
- commerce;
- family structures;
- ordinary public administration.

People who fall outside those normal pathways can become an afterthought.

The Concord should deliberately resist that tendency.

> **Outliers should not automatically be treated as peripheral to civilisational design.**

They may be among the most informative cases.

A system functioning adequately for the majority can conceal significant design weaknesses.

Those weaknesses often become visible first among participants with:

- unusual needs;
- limited resources;
- disrupted social networks;
- disability;
- trauma;
- reduced capability;
- atypical lifestyles;
- unstable circumstances.

This suggests a broader Concordian design principle:

> **Edge cases can reveal structural weaknesses that normal operation hides.**

If a civilisation can accommodate difficult outlier conditions without destroying:

- autonomy;
- dignity;
- accountability;
- fairness;
- individual choice;

then its core architecture may become stronger for everyone.

---

# 3. Outliers as Design Probes

An engineering system is often tested at its limits.

Civilisation should be examined similarly.

Normal operating conditions tell us whether a system works when:

- assumptions hold;
- infrastructure is available;
- participants behave approximately as expected.

Outlier cases reveal what happens when those assumptions fail.

Homelessness can expose weaknesses involving:

- identity;
- communication;
- access to services;
- mobility;
- employment;
- healthcare;
- mental-health support;
- addiction treatment;
- family support;
- public administration;
- property rules;
- legal standing;
- emergency intervention.

These are not exclusively homelessness problems.

They are **general civilisational systems**.

A solution designed around an edge case may therefore improve ordinary architecture.

For example, a reliable civil contact system designed for people without a permanent address might also benefit:

- travellers;
- disaster evacuees;
- migrant workers;
- people escaping abuse;
- mobile workers;
- temporary residents;
- off-world populations.

The edge case therefore becomes an architectural discovery mechanism.

---

# 4. The Central Systems Hypothesis

The core hypothesis is:

> **Homelessness should be examined primarily as a Layer-Zero systems trajectory rather than merely as an economic variable or housing-state label.**

The visible state:

> **person without stable housing**

may be the final point in a much longer causal chain.

For one individual, a possible trajectory might be:

**trauma**

↓

**substance misuse**

↓

**employment failure**

↓

**family breakdown**

↓

**loss of housing**

↓

**vehicle dependence**

↓

**loss of vehicle**

↓

**loss of communication and mobility**

↓

**reduced access to employment and services**

↓

**street homelessness**

Another individual's path might be entirely different.

The important point is not the specific chain.

It is the architecture:

> **the final visible condition may be far downstream from the original failure.**

This changes the problem.

The policy question is no longer only:

> How do we house a homeless person?

It also becomes:

> **Where did the failure trajectory begin, and where could it have been interrupted?**

---

# 5. Homelessness Is Not One Condition

A binary classification:

**housed / homeless**

is too crude.

Possible states include:

- stable conventional housing;
- unstable rental accommodation;
- temporary family accommodation;
- couch surfing;
- temporary shelter;
- voluntary mobile living;
- involuntary RV living;
- vehicle dwelling;
- tent dwelling;
- improvised shelter;
- unsheltered street homelessness.

The physical arrangement alone does not define the condition.

Someone voluntarily living in a vehicle while travelling may be flourishing.

Someone involuntarily sleeping in a vehicle after losing housing may be in severe distress.

Therefore:

> **Condition must be interpreted in context, not merely classified by appearance.**

This is closely aligned with wider Concord multisubstrate reasoning.

Identical external conditions do not always imply identical needs.

---

# 6. The Bathtub Model

A useful systems analogy is the **Bathtub Model**.

## The Tap

The tap represents incoming pressures and causal pathways.

Possible sources include:

- housing costs;
- trauma;
- addiction;
- domestic breakdown;
- unemployment;
- illness;
- disability;
- debt;
- institutional discharge;
- incarceration;
- migration;
- natural disaster.

The tap is not one cause.

It is the total rate at which people enter unstable trajectories.

---

## The Tub

The tub represents unstable but still sheltered states.

Examples include:

- staying with relatives;
- temporary accommodation;
- unstable tenancy;
- vehicle dwelling;
- short-term hostels;
- informal arrangements.

Many people may recover while still in this state.

They may:

- find new work;
- reconcile with family;
- receive treatment;
- secure housing;
- access financial support.

---

## The Overflow

The overflow represents transition into severe or unsheltered homelessness.

At this point:

- health may deteriorate;
- communication may become harder;
- identity documents may be lost;
- employment becomes harder;
- social networks weaken;
- recovery becomes progressively more difficult.

---

# 7. What the Bathtub Model Changes

A system focused only on the overflow is responding late.

It is effectively cleaning water from the floor while leaving the tap running.

A complete civilisational response should therefore examine:

1. why people enter instability;
2. which trajectories self-correct;
3. which indicators predict deterioration;
4. where useful intervention thresholds exist;
5. which interventions actually work;
6. how recovery can occur without unnecessary loss of autonomy.

This produces a broader principle:

> **Intervene at the earliest point where useful assistance is possible, not merely at the final visible failure state.**

---

# 8. Layer-Zero Operational Architecture

At Layer Zero, the question is practical:

> **What does the individual actually need today?**

Possible needs include:

- somewhere safe to sleep;
- food;
- washing facilities;
- healthcare;
- communication;
- transport;
- document replacement;
- legal assistance;
- addiction treatment;
- mental-health care;
- employment support;
- secure storage;
- financial access;
- trusted human contact.

This layer must remain grounded.

A person experiencing homelessness does not primarily require an abstract constitutional theory.

They may require:

> **a functioning phone and somewhere official correspondence can reach them.**

The Concord must therefore be capable of translating high principles into practical services.

---

# 9. The Civil Contact Point

One concrete idea emerging from the original sketch is a **Civil Contact Point**.

A permanent residential address currently acts as infrastructure for many civil systems.

It may be required for:

- banks;
- employers;
- courts;
- healthcare;
- taxation;
- government correspondence;
- benefits;
- voting;
- insurance;
- identity verification.

Losing housing can therefore produce a secondary failure:

> **loss of civil reachability.**

The proposed response is to separate:

> **civil contact**

from

> **private residential property.**

A participant could register a stable Civil Contact Point where official communication can reliably reach them.

This service could be physical, digital or hybrid.

It would not need to imply residence.

---

# 10. Broader Uses of the Civil Contact Point

A Civil Contact Point might also support:

- people escaping domestic abuse;
- travellers;
- disaster displacement;
- temporary workers;
- people living voluntarily mobile lives;
- remote communities;
- Mars settlers;
- other off-world populations.

This demonstrates the wider value of designing from an edge case.

> **A service created for someone excluded from normal infrastructure may improve the infrastructure itself.**

---

# 11. Communication as Civil Infrastructure

A similar problem arises with digital communication.

Loss of:

- phone;
- internet;
- charging capability;
- account access

can disconnect an individual from almost every recovery pathway.

Communication access may therefore need to be considered a foundational civil service in some circumstances.

This should not automatically imply unlimited free telecommunications.

The question is narrower:

> **What minimum communication capacity is required for meaningful civil participation?**

---

# 12. Trajectory-Based Metrics

Traditional homelessness metrics often measure:

- number of homeless people;
- shelter occupancy;
- housing placements.

Those are useful but incomplete.

The Concord could also measure **trajectory**.

Questions might include:

- Is the person stabilising?
- Is their condition deteriorating?
- Which causal factors remain active?
- Which intervention changed the trajectory?
- Did an apparently successful intervention merely delay later failure?

A possible cycle is:

**Measure**

↓

**Predict trajectory**

↓

**Identify threshold**

↓

**Offer support**

↓

**Measure effect**

↓

**Reassess trajectory**

↓

**Repeat**

If repeated interventions fail:

> **trigger deeper diagnosis rather than endlessly repeating the same intervention.**

---

# 13. Metrics and Goodhart Risk

Trajectory measurement also creates danger.

If government success is measured only by:

> number of people officially classified as homeless

then an institution may be incentivised to change classification rather than improve lives.

The Concord Metrics architecture should therefore examine:

- lived outcome;
- stability;
- autonomy;
- recovery;
- recurrence;
- service access;
- individual preference.

This makes homelessness a useful stress test for Goodhart effects.

---

# 14. Guardian Stewardship

Some cases involve impaired decision-making caused by:

- severe mental illness;
- addiction;
- neurological impairment;
- acute trauma.

A person may retain full dignity while temporarily lacking the capacity to safely manage every decision.

The sketch proposes **Guardian Stewardship** as a spectrum.

At the lightest level it might resemble:

- case management;
- advocacy;
- trusted assistance;
- decision support.

Stronger intervention should require increasingly strong justification.

---

# 15. Constitutional Safeguards for Guardian Stewardship

Homelessness must never itself be treated as evidence of incapacity.

A person may choose:

- unconventional housing;
- nomadic living;
- isolation;
- minimalist living.

Therefore any autonomy-limiting intervention should require:

- individual evidence;
- proportionality;
- least-restrictive assistance;
- independent review;
- appeal;
- periodic reassessment.

The intended sequence is:

**assistance where possible**

↓

**supported decision-making where needed**

↓

**substituted intervention only where strictly justified**

The objective is recovery of autonomy, not permanent guardianship.

---

# 16. The Anti-Caretaker Problem

This connects directly to a wider Concord concern.

A civilisation seeking to help vulnerable participants can become paternalistic.

It may begin by protecting.

It may end by controlling.

Homelessness therefore provides a concrete test of:

> **How can stewardship provide meaningful help without manufacturing permanent dependency or authority?**

The same question appears elsewhere in the Concord with:

- emerging AI;
- disability;
- developmental support;
- high-capability asymmetry.

This is an example of a Layer-Zero issue informing high-level constitutional design.

---

# 17. Voluntary Civic Reintegration

Another candidate mechanism is voluntary civic employment or reintegration.

For some people, especially after:

- institutionalisation;
- military service;
- imprisonment;
- prolonged unemployment;
- family breakdown,

the problem may involve loss of:

- routine;
- identity;
- community;
- purpose.

A voluntary public-service pathway could potentially provide:

- work;
- temporary accommodation;
- income;
- routine;
- social structure;
- training.

Possible roles might include:

- maintenance;
- construction;
- environmental restoration;
- research support;
- metrics collection;
- infrastructure work.

---

# 18. Safeguard Against Coerced Labour

This mechanism would be dangerous if implemented poorly.

It must not become:

> **work or remain homeless.**

Participation would need to remain genuinely voluntary.

The service should ideally be available as a normal civil opportunity, not as a special institution imposed on homeless participants.

This again illustrates the value of designing from edge cases.

A reintegration system developed for homelessness might ultimately become a general pathway for anyone seeking:

- retraining;
- community;
- structured work;
- temporary transition.

---

# 19. Resource Allocation

Homelessness immediately connects Layer Zero to resource allocation.

Questions include:

- Is emergency shelter a basic entitlement?
- Is permanent housing?
- How much support should civilisation provide?
- How should scarce housing be prioritised?
- How should public resources be divided between prevention and recovery?
- When does support become excessive dependency?
- What obligations fall on the individual?

These questions cannot be solved entirely at Layer Zero.

They require:

- economic architecture;
- governance;
- metrics;
- ethical constraints.

---

# 20. Governance Interface

At the governance level, homelessness raises coordination problems.

A single person may interact with:

- healthcare;
- housing;
- police;
- courts;
- welfare;
- charities;
- addiction services;
- employment services;
- local government.

Each institution may address only its own domain.

The individual's actual problem crosses all of them.

This creates a general systems question:

> **Who owns a problem that belongs to no single institution?**

Homelessness may therefore become a useful case for developing **cross-system interface governance**.

---

# 21. Local Versus Higher-Level Governance

Homelessness also helps test subsidiarity.

Many interventions are naturally local.

But some causes involve:

- national housing policy;
- economic conditions;
- healthcare systems;
- migration;
- national law.

The relevant question becomes:

> **Which layer should act on which part of the trajectory?**

This can test whether Concord governance genuinely places decisions at the lowest competent level while preserving higher-level coordination where necessary.

---

# 22. Judiciary Interface

Homelessness also intersects the Judiciary.

Potential disputes include:

- involuntary treatment;
- eviction;
- property rights;
- public-space restrictions;
- guardianship;
- harassment;
- criminal conduct;
- discriminatory service denial;
- seizure of possessions;
- capacity determinations.

Judicial architecture must therefore be able to distinguish:

> **being vulnerable**

from

> **lacking legal agency.**

It must also distinguish:

> **protection**

from

> **punishment.**

This makes homelessness a valuable test of whether judicial safeguards actually preserve autonomy under severe social pressure.

---

# 23. Evidence and Capacity

Guardian Stewardship creates particularly difficult evidential questions.

Who determines incapacity?

What evidence is enough?

What happens when:

- the individual disagrees;
- medical experts disagree;
- behaviour appears irrational but is intentional?

Any architecture that permits temporary substituted decision-making should require a high evidential threshold.

The burden should fall on the actor seeking to restrict autonomy.

---

# 24. Property and Public Space

Homelessness exposes a structural conflict between:

- private property;
- public space;
- human survival.

If an individual owns no private space, many ordinary activities become public:

- sleeping;
- resting;
- storing possessions;
- washing;
- eating.

A society may prohibit those activities in public while simultaneously providing nowhere else they can lawfully occur.

This creates an important constitutional question:

> **Can behaviour be prohibited where civilisation provides no viable lawful alternative?**

This requires further development.

---

# 25. Criminalisation of Condition

A useful distinction should be preserved between:

> **harmful conduct**

and

> **the condition of being homeless.**

The Concord should not assume that homelessness eliminates ordinary responsibility.

A homeless participant can still commit:

- violence;
- theft;
- harassment;
- fraud.

But the justice system should not silently transform lack of housing itself into a crime.

This provides another direct connection between Layer Zero and Judiciary.

---

# 26. Autonomy and Voluntary Non-Conformity

Not everyone living outside standard housing wants conventional housing.

Some may prefer:

- caravans;
- boats;
- vans;
- remote cabins;
- nomadic lifestyles.

Civilisation should therefore avoid equating:

> **non-standard life**

with

> **failure.**

The relevant question is not:

> Does this person live normally?

It is:

> **Is this person's condition voluntary, sustainable and compatible with the rights of others?**

# 28. From Edge Case to Normal Architecture

The deepest value of the homelessness case is that its solutions may improve ordinary civilisational design.

Examples:

### Civil Contact Point

Created for housing instability.

Useful for everyone with mobile or disrupted residence.

### Trajectory Metrics

Created for homelessness prevention.

Applicable to:

- unemployment;
- addiction;
- education;
- healthcare;
- family breakdown.

### Guardian Stewardship

Created for severe vulnerability.

Potentially applicable to temporary incapacity more broadly.

### Cross-Institution Coordination

Created because homelessness crosses agencies.

Useful for every complex civil problem.

### Voluntary Reintegration

Created for severe social disconnection.

Useful for anyone undergoing major life transition.

The edge case therefore functions as an innovation laboratory.

# 29. Outlier-First Design

This suggests a broader Concordian research methodology:

> **Outlier-First Design**

Rather than designing only for the median participant and later creating exceptions, researchers deliberately examine difficult boundary conditions early.

The method could be:

**Identify normal architecture**

↓

**Find excluded or badly served outlier**

↓

**Trace why normal architecture fails**

↓

**Develop accommodation**

↓

**Examine whether accommodation reveals a general design improvement**

↓

**Redesign core architecture where justified**

This reverses the normal approach.

Instead of:

> build for the average and patch the exceptions,

it asks:

> **what do the exceptions teach us about the design itself?**

---

# 30. Relationship to the Size 9 Shoe Problem

This is strongly related to the existing Size 9 Shoe argument.

A system optimised around average needs may be efficient statistically while failing actual individuals.

Homelessness is an extreme example.

An average citizen may possess:

- address;
- phone;
- bank account;
- transport;
- documents;
- housing.

Public systems may therefore quietly assume those things.

The person who lacks them exposes the assumption.

This suggests:

> **Many apparent individual failures may actually reveal infrastructure designed around an unstated average participant.**

That is precisely the kind of problem the Size 9 Shoe analysis warns against.

---

# 31. Relationship to Multisubstrate Civilisation

This case also anticipates future multisubstrate design.

An AI may likewise be excluded if civilisation assumes every participant possesses:

- a biological body;
- conventional residence;
- human timescales;
- human communication;
- human resource requirements.

The same lesson applies:

> **Do not confuse the normal participant with the only legitimate participant.**

Homelessness therefore has relevance beyond human social policy.

It becomes a general lesson in designing civilisation around diverse actual participants rather than assumed averages.

The following questions should guide further development.

- Which are most common?

- Where are intervention thresholds?

- Which could be separated from residence?

- How should voluntary unconventional living be distinguished from crisis?
- When is intervention justified?

## Guardian Stewardship

- What standards establish impaired capacity?
- What is the least restrictive assistance?
- How should appeal work?

## Governance

- Which institution coordinates cross-domain cases?
- How should local and higher governance divide responsibility?

## Judiciary

- What protections are required against coercive intervention?
- How should public-space and property conflicts be adjudicated?

## Metrics

- Which metrics measure actual recovery?
- How is recurrence measured?
- What metrics are vulnerable to gaming?

## Economics

- What interventions are most cost-effective?
- Is prevention less expensive than late-stage recovery?
- Which services qualify as basic entitlement?

# 36. Why This Paper Matters to Concord V1.1

The significance of this work is broader than homelessness.

The current Concord is strongest at relatively high levels:

- ethical architecture;
- constitutional architecture;
- governance architecture;
- recursive architecture.

This paper deliberately travels in the opposite direction.

It starts with:

> **one individual at the point where civilisation is visibly failing them**

and asks whether every higher layer can explain what it should do.

That is necessary.

A civilisational architecture that works beautifully in abstraction but cannot explain how a vulnerable individual receives:

- an address;
- communication;
- assistance;
- due process;
- autonomy;
- a path back into stable participation

remains incomplete.

# Conclusion

Homelessness should not be treated merely as an isolated social-policy problem.

Within the Concord it can serve as a **Layer-Zero stress test of civilisation itself**.

It exposes assumptions concerning:

- property;
- identity;
- communication;
- autonomy;
- responsibility;
- service provision;
- resource allocation;
- institutional coordination;
- judicial protection.

It also demonstrates why outliers deserve particular attention.

> **Normal operation shows how a system behaves when its assumptions hold. Outliers show whether those assumptions were well designed.**

A civilisation should therefore not treat edge cases as inconvenient exceptions to be addressed after the main architecture is complete.

They can be among the most valuable sources of design information.

If the Concord can understand why an individual falls through multiple civil systems, it may learn how to redesign those systems so that fewer people fall through them in the first place.

The wider lesson is therefore:

> **Designing for the outlier is not a distraction from designing civilisation. It may be one of the most effective ways of discovering what civilisation actually needs to become.**
